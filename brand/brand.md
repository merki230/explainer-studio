## Voice and tone

How we write comes from two reference scripts the owner chose, after rejecting the pilot as "sounds too AI ... adding detail just to make it longer ... I find this style annoying when watching a video" (2026-10-15). Excerpts, analysis and the recipe: `brand/research/script-references.md` (R§). Model text: R§5, the pilot redrafted in the new style (not fact-checked yet). The pilot's current `script.txt` is the counter-example.

| Rule | Value | Why | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Register | Calm, unhurried, confident. A guide showing you the inside of things, not a presenter. | Matches the calm, cut-open look; L§1 | 🧪 |
| No padding | **Every sentence adds new information**: a fact, name, place, number, step, or consequence. Padding test: delete the sentence; if the viewer loses nothing, it stays deleted. Never add detail to reach a length. | Owner: "adding detail just to make it longer" | ✅ owner feedback |
| No AI patterns | None of the banned patterns below. | Owner: "sounds too AI" | ✅ owner feedback |
| Sentences | Natural spoken sentences, **mostly 10 to 20 words**, joined the way people talk ("and", "because", "so"). A short sentence only to land a point, never two in a row. | R§1; the pilot's 4-to-6-word rhythm is what read as AI (R§4) | 🧪 |
| One story | Each video hangs on **one concrete event or moment** with a specific detail (who, where, when). The explanation attaches to it. | R§1 (the $70 million loan), R§2 (the 2022 strike) | 🧪 |
| Person | Talk **to** the viewer ("your phone", "your friend") when it is their experience. Narrate **like a reporter** (names, dates, past tense) when it is someone else's story. | R§3 | 🧪 |
| Point at the screen | When the explanation turns on something visible, name it: "Look at the router." At most 2 per Short, only for what is actually in frame. | R§2 ("Look at this map"); ties the voice to the frame | 🧪 |
| Attribute, don't hedge | Say who claims it, **in the voice, where the story needs it** (the only attribution the viewer gets in the video; § Sourcing policy): "researchers at Northeastern found", "FTC staff said", "one data company says". Hedge ("can", "often", "probably") **only where the source itself hedges**, and never in every sentence. A 🟡 claim is spoken with its attribution or the source's own hedge, never as the narrator's fact (`docs/sources-format.md`). | Owner disliked "a hedge in every sentence" (R§4); attribution is honest and sounds sure (R§2) | 🧪 |
| Company names | Name a company **only when the story is about its own product**: "Apple and Google say that check runs on the phone" (a wake-word beat about their assistants). **Never as a citation**: not "according to Acme", not a company named to borrow its authority for a claim about someone else. For citations, name the researchers, agency or kind of source ("researchers found", "FTC staff said", "one data company says") | The story sometimes is a company's product, and a vague "your phone's maker" sounds evasive; a company as a source reads as an ad or a footnote | ✅ owner decision (v0.9) |
| Numbers | **Any number that tells the story**, each ✅ sourced, said as the source says it (rounded only where the source's figure allows). No number for decoration. Replaces v0.4's "one number per video". | R§3; numbers are what the padding test keeps | 🧪 |
| Answer straight | When the question is asked, answer it in the next breath ("The answer is your Wi-Fi."). No teasing, no "stick around". | R§2 | 🧪 |
| Fairness | If the system has a fair side, say it once ("In fairness, ..."). | R§2; trust is the brand | 🧪 |
| Banned words | Exclamation marks. Hype: "insane", "you won't believe", "nobody talks about this", "went parabolic". Rhetorical "right?". "In our previous video". Any line spoken by a character, or addressed to one. | Hype breaks the calm register (R§1); the narrator is the only voice, the cast never speaks | 🧪 |

**Banned patterns.** 1 to 6 are what the owner disliked in the pilot (✅ owner feedback, R§4); 7 to 9 are the weak spots of the references (🧪, R§1, R§2).

1. Chains of fragments: "Same Wi-Fi. Same evening. Same couch."
2. Lists of three for rhythm: "Your phone. Your laptop. Your friend's phone. One home."
3. Fake reveals: "It feels like mind reading. It isn't."
4. Filler that doesn't move the explanation: "Now zoom out." "And that changes everything."
5. A hedge in every sentence: "often shares", "can look like", "can become", "can be enough".
6. Every sentence 4 to 6 words.
7. Signposts: "But here's the strange part." "And more importantly."
8. Slogan endings: "not X, but Y"; "the question isn't X, it's Y".
9. Loose comparisons with no source: "more than the GDP of some small island nations".

**Sounds AI vs sounds human** (pilot `script.txt` vs R§5; the new lines still need the fact-checker):

| Sounds AI (pilot) | Sounds human (new) |
|---|---|
| "You never searched it. Your friend did. Same Wi-Fi. Same evening. Same couch." | "Your friend spends the evening on your couch, scrolling coffee machines on her phone. The next morning, your phone shows you one." |
| "Every device on your Wi-Fi often shares the same public address. To an ad company, that can look like one household." | "Look at the router. Every phone and laptop behind it reaches the internet through the same public address, and the FTC says ad companies use that to work out which devices belong to one household." |
| "It feels like mind reading. It isn't. It doesn't need your voice. Sharing a Wi-Fi can be enough." | "So next time an ad seems to read your mind, think about who was on your Wi-Fi." |

**On-brand lines** (style examples; every claim in them still needs a ✅ source before it is spoken)

1. "In 2018, researchers at Northeastern University tested more than 17,000 Android apps for secret recording, and they didn't catch a single one sending audio."
2. "Look at the router. Every phone behind it reaches the internet through the same public address."
3. "Your friend spends the evening on your couch, scrolling coffee machines on her phone."
4. "So why does the light stay red when the road is empty? The answer is a sensor cut into the asphalt, and your car stopped just short of it."
5. "In fairness, the store isn't hiding this; it's in the terms you accepted when you signed up for the card."

**Off-brand lines**

1. "Your phone is SPYING on you and here's the proof!" (hype)
2. "Same Wi-Fi. Same evening. Same couch." (fragment chain)
3. "It feels like mind reading. It isn't." (fake reveal)
4. "Your devices can often share an address that can sometimes look like a household." (hedge in every clause)
5. "But here's the strange part, and it changes everything." (signpost, filler)

## Hook patterns

The hook is recipe steps 1 to 3 (§ Structure): the concrete moment, the surprising fact, the question. Proposition in the first 3 s, question asked by about 6 s (P§4, TikTok guidance; L§2). **The first frame is black: the phone screen of the story's moment waking** (the screen-bookend loop, § Structure, ✅ owner decision v0.8.2); by about 1 s the lock screen is lit, then the first app action, then the camera pulls back into the slice, wide by about 3 s. Never a title card or logo (L§2); the title block still enters at about 1 s. The opening line is spoken over the screen, so the screen must show the line's moment, not a generic unlock (the visual is the hook, L§2). Whether a black first frame costs swipe-aways is 🧪: watch the swipe-away column in `feedback.md`. Every hook is a full spoken sentence, never a fragment chain. All 🧪 until swipe-away numbers say otherwise.

| # | Pattern | Example first line | When to use |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **The lived moment** | "Your friend spends the evening on your couch, scrolling coffee machines on her phone. The next morning, your phone shows you one." | The surprise is something the viewer has lived (talk to them) |
| 2 | **The dated event** | "In March 2021, one ship stuck sideways in the Suez Canal held up world trade for six days." | A real event with a date and a number opens the system (narrate like a reporter, R§2) |
| 3 | **The role** | "Say you sign up for a store's loyalty card to save two dollars on a coffee." | Casting the viewer in a role makes the mechanism theirs (R§1, "So, you want to buy a ship") |
| 4 | **The finding** | "In 2018, researchers tested more than 17,000 Android apps for secret recording." | One sourced finding overturns what the viewer assumes; say who found it |
| 5 | **The wrong recipient** | "The barcode on your receipt isn't really there for you; it's for the store's stock system." | An everyday object has a hidden second audience |

Examples show the shape; each claim needs a ✅ source before use.

## Structure of a video

Every video follows the **7-step recipe** (R§3). **No word-count target: length follows the idea.** **Length** (✅ owner decision, 2026-10-15; replaces the hard 59 s cap): most Shorts land **around 30 to 60 s**; **over 60 s is allowed when the story needs it, provided the video has no licensed (Content ID-claimable) music**; music we make ourselves is fine (style-guide § 9 Licensing). Why: YouTube blocks any Short over 1 min with an active Content ID claim (P§3, S8), and our videos carry no third-party music. Revisit when TikTok Creator Rewards (videos ≥ 1 min, P§3, S19) is within reach. Long-form, later, uses the same steps with 2 or 3 questions, each answered in its own section. Scene change every 3 to 6 s, one idea per scene.

**One idea per frame** (✅ owner decision, 2026-10-15; details 🧪 in style-guide § 6). Besides the title block and the captions, at most **one** card on screen at a time (detail box, full-frame screen, callout, counter, or statement card), with empty space around the subject. No source line ever adds a second piece of text (§ Sourcing policy). Why: the owner's reference (kinetic type on black, CHANGELOG v0.6) reads clearer than our pilot because each frame carries one thing. At the 1 or 2 key moments (usually step 4's answer or step 5's idea to keep), the key phrase may appear as a **statement card** (style-guide § 6, ✅ owner decision).

**Several locations** (✅ owner decision, v0.8). A video visits as many slices as the story needs (a room at night, a car, a street cabinet, a data centre, the next morning's kitchen), and the system is shown as a **journey out of the room and back**, the camera following the orange from slice to slice (style-guide § 8). **The Lab** (✅ owner decision, v0.9.1) is the one recurring location: a cut-open test room where every "researchers found" beat is shown happening (a bench of phones, robot arms, a test rig, a counter and a wall meter built into the set), re-dressed for each study (style-guide § 3).

**Show it moving** (✅ owner decision, v0.9.1, from the pilot's rough-cut review: "we still see both mannequins, it's kind of boring; animation is also what people want to see"). Something meaningful moves on screen at least every 2 to 3 s; never two people sitting still under narration. Every beat that explains a mechanism gets a visual of that mechanism: an object cut open (the phone's wake-word chip), The Lab running the study, the orange travelling. Within a location the camera glides between framings; hard cuts are rare (style-guide § 8).

| Step | What the words do | What the frame does | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Open on something concrete | An event, or a moment the viewer has lived, with one specific detail | **The opening bookend** (✅ owner decision): first frame black, a full-frame phone screen wakes (black → lock screen → the first app action of the story), then the camera pulls back out of the screen into the slice, already lit, cast in place. The title block fades in at about 1 s. Name tags on first appearance in the slice | ✅ owner decision (bookend); 🧪 (the rest) |
| 2. The surprising fact | The thing the viewer didn't expect, attributed to whoever found it | No orange yet; at most a flicker on a screen (the tell). A detail box may show the screen. A finding by researchers is shown in The Lab | 🧪 |
| 3. The question | One question the video answers, asked by about 6 s (long-form: 2 or 3) | Hold on the object the question is about | 🧪 |
| 4. Answer, then mechanism | Answer right away, then walk through the mechanism step by step, in physical order, pointing at what's on screen ("Look at the router") | The orange appears and travels: device to device, then out through the wall and the ground, the camera following it to the next slice; the X-ray at most once. Each mechanism step has its own visual (an object cut-away, The Lab), never the cast sitting still under the explanation | ✅ owner decision (a visual per mechanism); 🧪 (the rest) |
| 5. One idea to keep | The one sentence the viewer could repeat to a friend | The trace stays on what the system touched; often the statement card | 🧪 |
| 6. Attribute, don't hedge | Throughout, not a separate part: who says so, in the sentence that claims it | Nothing: no source text on screen (✅ owner decision, v0.9); sources go in the post description | 🧪 (the words); ✅ (nothing on screen) |
| 7. Turn it back on the viewer | A consequence, or something to notice next time. Not a slogan | Back in the viewer's slice, then **the closing bookend** (✅ owner decision): the camera pushes back into a phone's screen, the phone locks (soft click), the screen goes black. Last frame black | ✅ owner decision (bookend); 🧪 (the rest) |

**The screen-bookend loop** (✅ owner decision, v0.8.2). Every video opens on a phone screen waking and ends on a phone locking; the first and last frames are both black, so the replay is seamless whatever the mood (Night or Day) of the first and last slices. Why: the pilot opened at Night and ended in Day, so the replay jumped; a screen bookend is mood-agnostic, reusable for every story, and frames each video on the phone, where the "secret" shows up. Replaces v0.8's loop (the last shot's phone locks and the cut lands on the room's first frame). Timings and camera: style-guide § 8.

**How a video is made** (✅ owner decision): rough cut → polish beat by beat → final pass, with the owner reviewing in Remotion Studio at each step. The steps and their order: `docs/playbook.md` § How a video is built.

Loop line (🧪): where it reads naturally, the last line also leads into the first (replays count as views on YouTube since 2025-03-31; completion is TikTok's strongest signal, P§4). Test: read the ending then the opening aloud. Never bend the ending into a slogan to make it loop.

## Sourcing policy

- Every claim follows `docs/sources-format.md`: ✅ verified, 🟡 plausible (spoken with its attribution or the source's own hedge, § Voice), ❌ unsupported (cut it), 🎭 illustrative (disclosed in the post description, below). The fact-checker must PASS before voice-over (CLAUDE.md pipeline).
- **Full sources go in the post description.** One line "Sources: …" plus "Full sources: link in profile" (P§6, P rules 14). YouTube Shorts description links are not clickable (P§6), so names, not URLs.
- **No source text on screen** (✅ owner decision, v0.9; retires v0.6's on-screen attribution line, so Open decision 5 now reads "never on screen" again). Why: in the build the line "felt like a footnote in the middle of a story". No attribution line, no "Source:", no URL, no document title in the picture. Sources go in the post description only.
- **Spoken attribution stays where the story needs it** ("researchers found", "FTC staff said"; § Voice, Attribute, don't hedge). A 🟡 claim is still spoken with its attribution or the source's hedge.
- **Company names in the voice** only when the story is about that company's own product, never as a citation (§ Voice, Company names; ✅ owner decision, v0.9). The named company's claim still needs its row in `sources.md` like any other.
- **Illustrative specifics (🎭) are disclosed off the picture** (✅ owner decision, 2026-10-15; replaces the on-screen `ILLUSTRATIVE` watermark). The post description carries one line, right after the hook line: "Illustrative: [what is invented]." e.g. "Illustrative: the couch, the friend and the coffee machine are an example, not a real case." The platform AI label is on as well (next bullet). Why: the watermark was a second piece of text competing with the subject (one idea per frame). Note: the platform labels say "AI-generated" / "altered or synthetic"; none of them says "illustrative" (P§6), so the description line is the actual disclosure and is never skipped.
- AI-voice label on, on all three platforms (P§6; Instagram lists "a reel narrated with a realistic AI-generated voiceover" as requiring a label). ✅ platform rule.
- Never invent a URL, a quote, or a study. A claim with no source does not get spoken.
