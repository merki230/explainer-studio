## Voice and tone

How we write comes from two reference scripts the owner chose, after rejecting the pilot as "sounds too AI ... adding detail just to make it longer ... I find this style annoying when watching a video" (2026-10-15). Excerpts, analysis and the recipe: `brand/research/script-references.md` (R§). Model text: R§5, the pilot redrafted in the new style (not fact-checked yet). The pilot's current `script.txt` is the counter-example.

| Rule | Value | Why | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Register | Calm, unhurried, confident. A guide in a quiet room, not a presenter. | Matches the museum; L§1 | 🧪 |
| No padding | **Every sentence adds new information**: a fact, name, place, number, step, or consequence. Padding test: delete the sentence; if the viewer loses nothing, it stays deleted. Never add detail to reach a length. | Owner: "adding detail just to make it longer" | ✅ owner feedback |
| No AI patterns | None of the banned patterns below. | Owner: "sounds too AI" | ✅ owner feedback |
| Sentences | Natural spoken sentences, **mostly 10 to 20 words**, joined the way people talk ("and", "because", "so"). A short sentence only to land a point, never two in a row. | R§1; the pilot's 4-to-6-word rhythm is what read as AI (R§4) | 🧪 |
| One story | Each video hangs on **one concrete event or moment** with a specific detail (who, where, when). The explanation attaches to it. | R§1 (the $70 million loan), R§2 (the 2022 strike) | 🧪 |
| Person | Talk **to** the viewer ("your phone", "your friend") when it is their experience. Narrate **like a reporter** (names, dates, past tense) when it is someone else's story. | R§3 | 🧪 |
| Point at the screen | When the explanation turns on something visible, name it: "Look at the router." At most 2 per Short, only for what is actually in frame. | R§2 ("Look at this map"); ties the voice to the exhibit | 🧪 |
| Attribute, don't hedge | Say who claims it: "researchers at Northeastern found", "the FTC says", "one data company says". Hedge ("can", "often", "probably") **only where the source itself hedges**, and never in every sentence. A 🟡 claim is spoken with its attribution or the source's own hedge, never as the narrator's fact (`docs/sources-format.md`). | Owner disliked "a hedge in every sentence" (R§4); attribution is honest and sounds sure (R§2) | 🧪 |
| Numbers | **Any number that tells the story**, each ✅ sourced, said as the source says it (rounded only where the source's figure allows). No number for decoration. Replaces v0.4's "one number per video". | R§3; numbers are what the padding test keeps | 🧪 |
| Answer straight | When the question is asked, answer it in the next breath ("The answer is your Wi-Fi."). No teasing, no "stick around". | R§2 | 🧪 |
| Fairness | If the system has a fair side, say it once ("In fairness, ..."). | R§2; trust is the brand | 🧪 |
| Banned words | Exclamation marks. Hype: "insane", "you won't believe", "nobody talks about this", "went parabolic". Rhetorical "right?". "In our previous video". Any line addressed to the mascot. | Hype breaks the museum (R§1); the mascot never speaks | 🧪 |

**Banned patterns.** 1 to 6 are what the owner disliked in the pilot (✅ owner feedback, R§4); 7 to 9 are the weak spots of the references (🧪, R§1, R§2).

1. Chains of fragments: "Same Wi-Fi. Same evening. Same couch."
2. Lists of three for rhythm: "Your phone. Your laptop. Your friend's phone. One home."
3. Fake reveals: "It feels like mind reading. It isn't."
4. Filler that doesn't move the explanation: "Now zoom out." "And that changes everything."
5. A hedge in every sentence: "often shares", "can look like", "can become", "can be enough".
6. Every sentence 4 to 6 words.
7. Signposts: "But here's the strange part." "And more importantly."
8. Slogan endings: "not X, but Y"; "the question isn't X, it's Y".
9. Loose comparisons with no source: "more than the GDP of some small island nations".

**Sounds AI vs sounds human** (pilot `script.txt` vs R§5; the new lines still need the fact-checker):

| Sounds AI (pilot) | Sounds human (new) |
|---|---|
| "You never searched it. Your friend did. Same Wi-Fi. Same evening. Same couch." | "Your friend spends the evening on your couch, scrolling coffee machines on her phone. The next morning, your phone shows you one." |
| "Every device on your Wi-Fi often shares the same public address. To an ad company, that can look like one household." | "Look at the router. Every phone and laptop behind it reaches the internet through the same public address, and the FTC says ad companies use that to work out which devices belong to one household." |
| "It feels like mind reading. It isn't. It doesn't need your voice. Sharing a Wi-Fi can be enough." | "So next time an ad seems to read your mind, think about who was on your Wi-Fi." |

**On-brand lines** (style examples; every claim in them still needs a ✅ source before it is spoken)

1. "In 2018, researchers at Northeastern University tested more than 17,000 Android apps for secret recording, and they didn't catch a single one sending audio."
2. "Look at the router. Every phone behind it reaches the internet through the same public address."
3. "Your friend spends the evening on your couch, scrolling coffee machines on her phone."
4. "So why does the light stay red when the road is empty? The answer is a sensor cut into the asphalt, and your car stopped just short of it."
5. "In fairness, the store isn't hiding this; it's in the terms you accepted when you signed up for the card."

**Off-brand lines**

1. "Your phone is SPYING on you and here's the proof!" (hype)
2. "Same Wi-Fi. Same evening. Same couch." (fragment chain)
3. "It feels like mind reading. It isn't." (fake reveal)
4. "Your devices can often share an address that can sometimes look like a household." (hedge in every clause)
5. "But here's the strange part, and it changes everything." (signpost, filler)

## Hook patterns

The hook is recipe steps 1 to 3 (§ Structure): the concrete moment, the surprising fact, the question. Proposition in the first 3 s, question asked by about 6 s (P§4, TikTok guidance; L§2). The first frame is a striking image of the exhibit, never a title card (L§2). Every hook is a full spoken sentence, never a fragment chain. All 🧪 until swipe-away numbers say otherwise.

| # | Pattern | Example first line | When to use |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **The lived moment** | "Your friend spends the evening on your couch, scrolling coffee machines on her phone. The next morning, your phone shows you one." | The surprise is something the viewer has lived (talk to them) |
| 2 | **The dated event** | "In March 2021, one ship stuck sideways in the Suez Canal held up world trade for six days." | A real event with a date and a number opens the system (narrate like a reporter, R§2) |
| 3 | **The role** | "Say you sign up for a store's loyalty card to save two dollars on a coffee." | Casting the viewer in a role makes the mechanism theirs (R§1, "So, you want to buy a ship") |
| 4 | **The finding** | "In 2018, researchers tested more than 17,000 Android apps for secret recording." | One sourced finding overturns what the viewer assumes; say who found it |
| 5 | **The wrong recipient** | "The barcode on your receipt isn't really there for you; it's for the store's stock system." | An everyday object has a hidden second audience |

Examples show the shape; each claim needs a ✅ source before use.

## Structure of a video

Every video follows the **7-step recipe** (R§3). **No word-count target: length follows the idea.** A Short runs **about 25 to 45 s** and never past 59 s (Open decision 3). Long-form, later, uses the same steps with 2 or 3 questions, each answered in its own section. Scene change every 3 to 6 s, one idea per scene.

| Step | What the words do | What the exhibit does | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Open on something concrete | An event, or a moment the viewer has lived, with one specific detail | First frame: the exhibit, plaster, already lit. The exhibit label fades in at about 1 s. | 🧪 |
| 2. The surprising fact | The thing the viewer didn't expect, attributed to whoever found it | Still plaster; at most a flicker on a screen (the tell) | 🧪 |
| 3. The question | One question the video answers, asked by about 6 s (long-form: 2 or 3) | Hold on the object the question is about | 🧪 |
| 4. Answer, then mechanism | Answer right away, then walk through the mechanism step by step, in physical order, pointing at what's on screen ("Look at the router") | The orange appears and travels: the hidden system draws itself across the room over 2 to 3 s, light bleeds onto the plaster | 🧪 |
| 5. One idea to keep | The one sentence the viewer could repeat to a friend | Stains spread to what the system touched. Optional: the night guard walks in, looks | 🧪 |
| 6. Attribute, don't hedge | Throughout, not a separate part: who says so, in the sentence that claims it | None | 🧪 |
| 7. Turn it back on the viewer | A consequence, or something to notice next time. Not a slogan | The guard clicks the torch off; the room goes back to plaster with its stains | 🧪 |

Loop (🧪): where it reads naturally, the last line leads into the first (replays count as views on YouTube since 2025-03-31; completion is TikTok's strongest signal, P§4). Test: read the ending then the opening aloud. Never bend the ending into a slogan to make it loop.

## Mascot: the night guard 🧪

| Rule | Value | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Who | A small plaster figure, faceless, same material as everything else, with a torch. The museum's night guard. Silent. | A witness, not a host (L§3, Kurzgesagt's birds). Same material keeps the orange rule intact |
| What the torch does | Its beam can be the orange reveal: where it points, the hidden system shows and the plaster stains. Torch **click-on** and **click-off** are the audio sting and the loop point. | Gives the brand sting a reason to exist in the story; a sting at the end, never at the start (L§3) |
| First 3 s | The figure never appears in the first 3 s. The beam alone may. | L§3: a mascot before the payoff wastes the 3-second window |
| Speech | Never speaks, never has a caption, never gets a name on screen. | Keeps the voice-over the only voice |
| Screen time | At most 2 appearances per video, each ≤ 4 s, always in the lower-left or lower-right third of the safe area, never covering the focal action | Keeps him a witness |
| Running gag (optional) | He notices the stain, checks it with the torch, and it is still there when the light goes off. | Rewards returning viewers without blocking new ones (L§3) |
| Size | About 1/3 of a seated adult figure in the scene (head radius ≈ 0.06 units vs 0.165 for people). | Reads as a mascot, not a third person |

**Alternative: no mascot.** The museum (plaster + orange + exhibit numbers) is already a world (L§3: Nutshell Animations' palette). If the guard tests badly (comments calling him cute or distracting, or no change in completion), drop him and keep only the torch click as the sting. Decide after 5 exhibits with and 5 without.

## Naming of exhibits 🧪

| Rule | Value | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Format | `No. 014 · The Living Room` | Mock-up value; reads as a museum label |
| Number | Three digits, zero-padded, in publication order. Starts at 001. Never reused, even if a video is pulled. | Makes the catalogue browsable; a gap is honest |
| The pilot | `phone-listening` is **No. 001**. Numbering starts there, in publication order. ✅ owner decision, 2026-10-15 | The first exhibit sets the count; "No. 014" in the rows above and in the style guide is a mock-up value, not the pilot's number |
| Title | The place or object where the hidden system lives, 1 to 3 words, Title Case, usually "The …". The living room, the receipt, the elevator, the loyalty card. Never the system itself ("Cross-device tracking") and never a question. | The title is the exhibit; the system is the surprise |
| Sub-line (on the exhibit label) | `EXHIBIT · EVENING` style: the word EXHIBIT, a middle dot, then one short context that names a **place, time or category**, uppercase, ≤ 18 characters. It **stays the same across consecutive scenes of the same type** (what the shot is about: the phone, the evening room, the household, the scale; not the scene component, so 6a `profiles` and 6b `room` share `ONE HOME`) and changes only where the type changes. No. 001: scenes 1 to 3 `A PHONE`, 4a to 5b `EVENING`, 6a to 7 `ONE HOME`, 8 `SCALE`, 9a `A PHONE`, 9b `EVENING` | A sub-line that changes on every cut draws over the old one mid-dissolve (style-guide § 7); one context per stretch reads as a museum room, not a caption |
| Label timing | The first scene's label enters at **≈ 1 s** (`chip.delay: 1.0`), never in the first second; every other scene's label enters with its scene. The title stays the same through the whole video | § Structure, Hook: the first frame is the exhibit, not text |
| Video slug | `src/videos/<slug>/` keeps its plain-English slug (`phone-listening`); the exhibit number lives in `spec.ts` / `publish.md` | Slugs are for the team; numbers are for viewers |
| Platform title | YouTube title ≤ 100 chars, hook in the first 40, number optional at the end: "Why your phone shows ads for things your friend searched · No. 014" | P§3 title limit; P rules 13 |

## Sourcing policy

- Every claim follows `docs/sources-format.md`: ✅ verified, 🟡 plausible (spoken with its attribution or the source's own hedge, § Voice), ❌ unsupported (cut it), 🎭 illustrative (watermark on). The fact-checker must PASS before voice-over (CLAUDE.md pipeline).
- **Sources go in the post description, never on screen.** Owner's choice (Open decision 5). One line "Sources: …" plus "Full sources: link in profile" (P§6, P rules 14). YouTube Shorts description links are not clickable (P§6), so names, not URLs.
- The `ILLUSTRATIVE` watermark stays on any video that shows an invented specific (CLAUDE.md). It is the one piece of on-screen text that is not an exhibit label, caption, or callout.
- AI-voice label on, on all three platforms (P§6; Instagram lists "a reel narrated with a realistic AI-generated voiceover" as requiring a label). ✅ platform rule.
- Never invent a URL, a quote, or a study. A claim with no source does not get spoken.

## What we never do

| Never | Why | Status |
|---|---|---|
| A second accent colour. Orange is the only colour in the frame, whatever the system (data, money, heat). | One colour is the identity; multi-accent was the rejected navy direction | 🧪 |
| Clipped whites. Measured per style-guide § 2: p95 ≤ 90%, brightest plaster ≤ 96%, no pixel at 255. Brightness is not raised from the built values. | Owner rejected a brighter version as "too bright", then approved the built renders on 2026-10-15 (p95 ≈ 88%, brightest plaster 94.9 to 95.7%, nothing at 255) | ✅ owner feedback (the look); 🧪 thresholds |
| A title card, logo sting, or the guard in the first 3 s. | L§3: branded intros hurt swipe rate | 🧪 |
| A mascot that talks, or a script line addressed to it. | Silent witness only | 🧪 |
| Realistic humans or faces. Figures are faceless plaster. | L§4: sidestep the uncanny and the AI-slop association | 🧪 |
| In-app library music baked into the master. | P§5: not licensed across platforms; Content ID claims block Shorts over 1 min | ✅ platform rule |
| Readable text outside x 120 to 888, y 288 to 1248. | P§1 combined safe zone | ✅ platform rule |
| On-screen source citations or URLs. | Owner's choice (Open decision 5) | 🧪 |
| Re-uploading a file downloaded from another app. | P§4: Instagram reduces reach for other apps' watermarks | ✅ platform rule |
| An exhibit that reuses the previous exhibit's room, props and script structure unchanged. New room, new props, new system every time; same material and light. | P§4 and L§4: YouTube's "inauthentic content" policy targets templated AI content | ✅ platform rule |
| A claim or a number without a ✅ source (a 🟡 claim only with attribution or the source's hedge). | Trust is the brand | 🧪 |
