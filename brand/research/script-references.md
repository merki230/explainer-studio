## R§1 Reference 1: "So, you want to buy a ship" (excerpts)

> So, you want to buy a ship, or at the very least, you want to know what actually happens after you
> wire the money and take ownership. First of all, a modern cargo ship can cost anywhere from $120
> million to even $150 million. [...] But here's the strange part. The company operating the ship
> usually doesn't own it. And more importantly, how does owning a $150 million floating asset actually
> make you money? Because in shipping, ownership is a completely different business than operation,
> and it's far riskier than it looks.
> [...] These owners don't move cargo. They don't care about the logistics of getting a PlayStation
> from Shenzhen to Los Angeles. They just rent their ships.
> [...] Let's say you borrow $70 million to buy a $100 million ship. A year later, the market crashes
> and the resale value drops to 50 million. You are now underwater on your loan. [...] If you can't
> pay, they seize the fleet.
> [...] Instead, they create 50 separate shell companies, often registered in Liberia, the Marshall
> Islands, or Panama. One company, one ship. Why? Because if a ship spills oil or goes hopelessly
> bankrupt, the rest of the fleet is safe.
> [...] (ending) you begin to understand how modern maritime empires are built, not through control of
> the cargo, but through the relentless calculation of risk.

What works: casts the viewer in a role ("you want to buy a ship"; "you borrow $70 million"); one
driving question carries the video and each section answers part of it; every sentence carries a
number, name, place, or consequence (padding test); concrete over abstract (a PlayStation from
Shenzhen to LA; the Ever Given); natural spoken sentences of 10–20 words with an occasional short one
to land a point; real tension (boom/bust, early twist, stakes).
Avoid: hype words ("went parabolic", "generational wealth", "corpses of companies"); signposts ("But
here's the strange part", "And more importantly"); the "not X, but Y" ending; loose unsourced facts
("more than the GDP of some small island nations").

## R§2 Reference 2: Felixstowe, "the most important door into Britain" (excerpts)

> In August 2022, about 1,900 workers at a port walked out for 8 days, and the effects of that strike
> rippled across the country. The port is Felixstowe. On a normal day, it handles somewhere between 40
> and 48% of all the containers entering or leaving the United Kingdom. And it isn't owned by the
> British government. It's owned by a Hong Kong company. So, how did a Victorian seaside town become
> the most important door into Britain? Who exactly owns it, and what happens to a country when almost
> half of its trade passes through one gate?
> [...] So, why did it win? The answer is geography and timing. Look at this map. Felixstowe sits on
> the east coast [...] facing the North Sea.
> [...] Let's zoom in, because how Felixstowe operates is exactly what makes it so important. A giant
> ship arrives from Asia and pulls up to the berth. Then enormous cranes lift boxes off its deck. Each
> container is scanned, logged, and placed in a stack in the yard. Then some containers go onto trucks
> and many go onto rails.
> [...] Efficiency and resilience are opposites. Felixstowe is valuable because everything is
> concentrated in one place [...]. That's cheap and it's fast. But that same concentration means that
> when this one spot stumbles, the whole country feels it.
> [...] According to reports, the deal included Felixstowe [...] Cosco reportedly wanted a stake [...]
> As of the latest reports that we found, the sale of the remaining ports hadn't been finally settled.
> [...] In fairness, a privately owned port isn't automatically bad for Britain. [...]
> But the question isn't whether the port is well-run, it's what happens when it isn't.

What works: cold open on a real event with a date and number, then the surprising fact; the questions
stated up front, each answered directly ("So why did it win? The answer is geography and timing.");
narration points at the visuals ("Look at this map", "Let's zoom in"); the mechanism walked through
step by step in physical order; one big idea to keep ("efficiency and resilience are opposites");
**attribution instead of hedging** ("according to reports", "reportedly", "as of the latest reports
we found"), which is honest and confident; fairness ("In fairness, ...").
Avoid: references to "our previous videos"; repetition; the "the question isn't X, it's Y" ending
(earned here, but a crutch).

## R§3 The combined recipe (main session's proposal, owner approved "go")

1. Open on something concrete: an event, or a moment the viewer has lived, with one specific detail.
2. Say the surprising fact.
3. Ask the question the video answers (one for a Short; two or three for long-form).
4. Answer it right away, then show the mechanism step by step, pointing at what's on screen.
5. Leave one idea the viewer keeps.
6. Attribute, don't hedge ("researchers found", "the FTC says").
7. End by turning it back on the viewer: a consequence or something to notice, not a slogan.
Talk *to* the viewer when it's their experience; narrate like a reporter when it's a story.
Numbers: any that tell the story, each sourced (replaces "one number per video").
No word-count target: length follows the idea (Shorts ≈ 25–45 s; since brand v0.7, ≈ 30–60 s, over 60 s allowed without licensed music, brand.md § Structure).

## R§4 AI patterns the owner disliked in the pilot (to ban)
- Chains of fragments: "Same Wi-Fi. Same evening. Same couch." / "Your phone. Your laptop. Your
  friend's phone. One home."
- Lists of three for rhythm.
- Fake reveals: "It feels like mind reading. It isn't."
- Filler sentences that don't move the explanation.
- A hedge in every sentence ("can", "often", "probably").
- Every sentence 4–6 words.

## R§5 Draft pilot in the new style (not fact-checked)
> Your friend spends the evening on your couch, scrolling coffee machines on her phone. The next
> morning, your phone shows you one. You never searched for it.
> So is your phone listening? In 2018, researchers at Northeastern University tested more than 17,000
> Android apps for secret recording. They didn't catch a single one sending audio.
> The answer is your Wi‑Fi. Look at the router. Every phone and laptop behind it reaches the internet
> through the same public address, and the FTC says ad companies use that to work out which devices
> belong to one household.
> Last night, to them, you and your friend were one home. So next time an ad seems to read your mind,
> think about who was on your Wi‑Fi.
