## Description (YouTube)

Why do many hybrids get better mileage in the city?

Look at the window sticker of a gas car and its hybrid twin. One of them flips.

Is your hybrid's mileage really better in town, even in winter?

Illustrative: the city corner, the two cars, the lead cars and the drive are an example, not a real trip. The sticker card is a generic design, not the real EPA label; its figures are real 2026 base-trim ratings (see below). The 35 to 70 mph run and the size of the arrow are drawn for the story. The brake glow, the energy paths and the slowed engine and motor are drawings of the energy, not measurements. The winter scene and the weaker regenerative braking are a picture of "battery and regenerative braking perform worse in the cold", not a measured amount.

Gas cars usually rate higher on the highway than in the city, because the city means idling and braking. Many hybrids flip that: they switch the engine off at a stop, can pull away on the electric motor, and turn some braking energy back into electricity. On the highway there is little idling or braking to save, so the hybrid's edge shrinks, but it still beats its gas version.

Sticker figures shown: 2026 Honda Accord (gas, 1.5 L) 29 city / 37 highway mpg and 2026 Honda Accord Hybrid 51 city / 44 highway mpg, base trims, from fueleconomy.gov (EPA and DOE). Other trims differ. The cold-weather figure is the site's: in city driving at 20°F, hybrid fuel economy can drop about 30% to 34% compared with 77°F. Ratings are test results; your mileage will vary.
