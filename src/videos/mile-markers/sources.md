# Sources: mile-markers

Researcher draft, 2026-10-26, fact-checked 2026-10-26 against the 9-line first-draft script. The "Script claims" section below is the fact-checker's line-by-line result and wins over the researcher's older entries where they differ. Entries A-G are the researcher's evidence; their "Script (likely)" lines are not the real script.

Owner-approved angle: the interstate is a giant ruler. (1) Mile markers count up from the state's south or west border. (2) In most states, exit numbers use the same count, so you can subtract two exit numbers to get the miles between them. (3) When you're stranded and call 911, the marker beside you is how help finds you. Story: one driver on a road trip passes markers and exits, breaks down at night with no landmark, calls 911, reads the marker, and help arrives at that spot.

## Key facts in one screen

1. **Every interstate (a freeway) must have mile markers.** MUTCD 2H.11 (Standard) says they "shall be placed … on all freeway facilities". The same Standard sentence gives their purpose, "to provide a means for identifying the location of emergency incidents and traffic crashes".
2. **Zero is at the south or west.** MUTCD 2H.11 (Standard): "Zero distance shall begin at the south and west State lines, or at the south and west terminus points where routes begin within a State." Divided highways are measured on the northbound or eastbound side, and the southbound or westbound markers sit "directly opposite".
3. **Exit numbers must use the mile count.** MUTCD 2E.22 (Standard): "Interchange exit numbering shall use the reference location sign exit numbering method. The consecutive exit numbering method shall not be used. The exit numbers shall correspond to the posted Reference Location or Enhanced Reference Location signs."
4. **FHWA spells out the "giant ruler" and 911 ideas.** MUTCD 2E.22 (Support) says mileage-based exit numbers assist "road users in determining their destination distances and travel mileage, assists road users in reporting their location in the event of an incident or breakdown, assists responders in responding to incidents".
5. **No deadline to convert.** The 11th Edition's target compliance dates (Table 1B-1) don't list exit numbering. Old sequential signs get replaced as part of "systematic upgrading", or when they're no longer serviceable (1B.03). That's why a few Northeastern states are still converting: CT schedules I-84 and I-91 for 2027 and I-95 for 2028, NY is going corridor by corridor, and NH hasn't started.
6. **Tenth-mile markers exist.** They're optional, at "one tenth of a mile, two tenths of a mile, or one-half mile intervals". The "enhanced" version stacks direction, route shield, MILE, number, and the tenth.
7. **Phones send a location too.** FCC rules require carriers to locate 80% of wireless 911 calls to within 50 meters (or give a dispatchable location). So the marker is not the *only* way help finds you, but highway agencies tell drivers to give it, and they say it matters most in rural areas without landmarks.

## B. Mile markers (MUTCD calls them "Reference Location signs")

### B1. Required on freeways; purpose; zero at the south/west; one shared count for both directions

- **Script (likely):** "Every interstate has a little green sign every mile. The count starts at zero where the highway enters from the south or west, and it climbs all the way across the state."
- **Status:** ✅ (PDF). Section numbers are for the 11th Edition. In the 2009 edition this was 2H.05, and the old HTML still shows it that way.
- **Source:** [MUTCD 11th Edition, Chapters 2H–2N](https://mutcd.fhwa.dot.gov/pdfs/11th_Edition/Chapter2hthu2n.pdf), FHWA, 2023-12, Section 2H.11, p. 466–467 (accessed 2026-10-26)
- **Quote (PDF), Standard, Par. 02:** "Except when Enhanced Reference Location signs (see Section 2H.12) are used instead, Reference Location (D10-1 through D10-3) signs shall be placed on all expressway facilities that are located on a route where there is reference location sign continuity and on all freeway facilities to assist road users in estimating their progress, to provide a means for identifying the location of emergency incidents and traffic crashes, and to aid in highway maintenance and servicing."
- **Quote (PDF), Standard, Par. 10:** "For divided highways, the distance measurement shall be made on the northbound and eastbound roadways. The reference location signs for southbound or westbound roadways shall be set at locations directly opposite the reference location signs for the northbound or eastbound roadways."
- **Quote (PDF), Standard, Par. 11:** "Zero distance shall begin at the south and west State lines, or at the south and west terminus points where routes begin within a State."
- **Quote (PDF), Standard, Par. 07 (first sentence):** "The distance numbering shall be continuous for each route within a State, except where overlaps occur (see Section 2E.22)."
- **Note:** "Within a State" means the count restarts at every state line. Placement can shift: Option Par. 14 says a sign "may be moved in either direction as much as 50 feet", and Guidance Par. 15 says it "should be omitted" if it can't be placed within 50 feet. So "a marker every mile" is right in practice, but a marker can be missing or slightly off.
- **State-level confirmation (snippet, lead only):** ADOT blog "Marking miles, one milepost at a time", Caroline Carpenter, 2017-06-13. The search summary says Interstate mileposts "begin at zero at the western state line". URL https://azdot.gov/adot-blog/marking-miles-one-milepost-time returned 403.

### B2. Overlaps: only one route keeps its count, and the interstate wins

- **Script (likely):** Probably not spoken. Use it for the "where it breaks" caveat or a comment reply.
- **Status:** ✅ (PDF).
- **Source:** MUTCD 11th Ed., Section 2H.11 (as B1)
- **Quote (PDF), Standard, Par. 07:** "Where routes overlap, reference location sign continuity shall be established for only one of the routes. If one of the overlapping routes is an Interstate route, that route shall be selected for continuity of distance numbering."

## C. Exit numbers

### C1. Exit numbers must match the mile markers; sequential numbering is not allowed; FHWA's stated reasons

- **Script (likely):** "And the exit numbers use the same count. Exit 150 sits near mile marker 150."
- **Status:** ✅ (PDF). This is the core of the angle and it is a Standard ("shall").
- **Source:** [MUTCD 11th Edition, Chapter 2E](https://mutcd.fhwa.dot.gov/pdfs/11th_Edition/Chapter2e.pdf), FHWA, 2023-12, Section 2E.22 "Interchange Exit Numbering", p. 304–312 (accessed 2026-10-26). In the 2009 edition this was 2E.31, which CTDOT's page still cites.
- **Quote (PDF), Standard, Par. 01:** "Interchange exit numbering shall use the reference location sign exit numbering method. The consecutive exit numbering method shall not be used. The exit numbers shall correspond to the posted Reference Location or Enhanced Reference Location signs."
- **Quote (PDF), Support, Par. 02:** "Reference location sign exit numbering assists road users in determining their destination distances and travel mileage, assists road users in reporting their location in the event of an incident or breakdown, assists responders in responding to incidents, and assists highway agencies because the exit numbering sequence does not have to be changed if new interchanges are added to a route."
- **Quote (PDF), Standard, Par. 04 (first sentence):** "Interchange exit numbering shall be used in signing each freeway interchange exit."
- **Quote (PDF), Standard, Par. 15:** "Regardless of whether a mainline route originates within a State or crosses into the State from an adjacent State, the southernmost or westernmost terminus within that State shall be the beginning point for interchange exit numbering."
- **Quote (PDF), Standard, Par. 20:** "Where numbered routes overlap, continuity of interchange exit numbering shall be established for only one of the routes (see Figure 2E-8). If one of the routes is an Interstate and the other route is not an Interstate, the Interstate route shall maintain continuity of exit interchange numbering."
- **Note:** The Support paragraph is the best official line for both halves of the story (distances *and* reporting a breakdown). It is "Support", so it's informational, not a rule. Say "federal guidance says" or "the rulebook says", not "the law requires", when quoting the *reasons*. The bonus reason is good: new exits don't force renumbering.

### C2. How a number is picked: the mile at the crossroad, A/B for two exits in one mile

- **Script (likely):** "If the road crosses between mile 150 and 151, that's Exit 150. Two exits in the same mile? 150A and 150B."
- **Status:** ✅ (PDF). The examples are Guidance ("should… to the extent practical"), so the script says "about".
- **Source:** MUTCD 11th Ed., Section 2E.22 (as C1)
- **Quote (PDF), Guidance, Par. 07:** "To the extent practical, exit numbering should be determined based upon the location of the crossroad with respect to reference location signs as given in the following examples: A. If a crossroad intersects the mainline approximately at or after Mile 15 and before Mile 16, the interchange should be designated as EXIT 15"
- **Quote (PDF), Standard, Par. 05:** "Suffix letters shall only be used to supplement exit numbers where there is more than one exit associated with the reference mile points of the freeway." … "The suffix letters assigned shall be in ascending alphabetical order starting with the letter A for ramps in the direction of travel with increasing exit numbers, and in descending alphabetical order ending in the letter A in the opposite direction of travel."
- **Quote (PDF), Standard, Par. 06:** "if an exit is designated as EXIT 256 A, then there shall not be an exit designated as EXIT 256 on the same route in the same direction."
- **Quote (PDF), Standard, Par. 05 (cardinal letters):** "Exit numbers shall not include the cardinal direction initials corresponding to the directions of the cross route."
- **Note:** A good small surprise: driving south, you meet 150B *before* 150A. A mile with no interchange simply has no exit number. Figure 2E-3 shows Exit 10, then 12, 15, 18, with numbers skipped. Exits like "15E/15W" (NH I-93, per Wikipedia) break the cardinal-letter rule and are legacy signs; don't feature them.

### C3. Required, but no deadline: conversion happens as signs wear out

- **Script (likely):** Possibly an aside: "Federal rules now require it, but there's no deadline, so a few states are still swapping signs."
- **Status:** ✅ (PDF) for "no target compliance date listed" and the replace-when-worn rule. "There is no deadline" is my reading of Table 1B-1 (exit numbering isn't on it) plus 1B.03; it's not a sentence in the source. Fair as worded with "no deadline".
- **Source:** MUTCD 11th Ed., Part 1 (as A1), Section 1B.03 and Table 1B-1, p. 5–6
- **Quote (PDF), Standard, 1B.03 Par. 05:** "Unless a particular device is no longer serviceable (see definition in Section 1C.02), non-compliant devices on existing highways and bikeways shall be brought into compliance with the current edition of the National MUTCD as part of the systematic upgrading of substandard traffic control devices"
- **Quote (PDF), Standard, 1B.03 Par. 12:** "A non-compliant traffic control device that is being replaced or refurbished because it is damaged, missing, or no longer serviceable (see definition in Section 1C.02) for any reason shall be replaced with a compliant device"
- **Note:** Table 1B-1 lists six compliance-dated items (weight limit, low clearance, pavement marking retroreflectivity, two rail-crossing items). Exit numbering is not among them (PDF, read the full table). Secondary history (Wikipedia, lead only): the 2009 edition made mileage-based numbering the standard without adopting a compliance date.

### C4. "Most states": who still uses sequential numbers

- **Script (likely):** "On most interstates…" (hook). Don't give a count.
- **Status:** 🟡. "Most" is safe: the MUTCD requires it (C1), and every source agrees the holdouts are a handful of Northeastern states. I found **no official national count**. Current holdouts (2026), assembled from sources of mixed quality:
  - **Connecticut**, converting: ✅ (tool, official). [CTDOT Exit Renumbering](https://portal.ct.gov/dot/traffic-engineering/exit-renumbering), CT DOT, undated page (© 2026), accessed 2026-10-26. Quote (tool): "The exits are being renumbered to conform to Federal standards." / "Exit numbers will be determined based on the mileage contained in the CTDOT Linear Referencing System." Schedule (tool): I-84 2027, I-91 2027, I-95 2028; CT-2, CT-15 and I-291 completed 2025; I-395 completed 2015. **Fact-checker re-opened 2026-10-26: schedule ✅ (verified directly; page says "the schedule is subject to change").** Also CT-8 and I-384 completed 2024, CT-9 and I-691 completed 2023. Corrected quotes: "CTDOT has decided not to use Exit 0 to reduce potential confusion." (the earlier "There is no Exit 0" wording was not on the page I read), "The Federal Highway Administration allows Exit 0 but does not require its use.", rounding "Round down to the nearest whole mile" from mile 3.00, and "CTDOT may round up or down to the next nearest whole mile to reduce suffix letters or utilize the existing exit number."
  - **New York**, converting corridor by corridor: 🟡. News reports (WBNG 2024-06-17; WAER 2025-04-07; snippets) cover I-81, I-481 and I-690 in Central NY. Example (snippet): I-81's "current Exit 9 (Marathon) … will become Exit 38". Not opened; re-check if used.
  - **New Hampshire**, still sequential: 🟡 (snippets). Legislative testimony on HB 1785 (2024) says the renumbering project keeps getting dropped from the Ten-Year Plan and that federal waivers "have always been approved". The testimony page failed to load (parse error). The NHPR 2017 snippet says Concord's Exit 14 sits at about mile 38.
  - **Delaware** (I-95, I-495) and **Vermont**: 🟡 (Wikipedia / Gribblenation 2022, leads only).
  - **Massachusetts** converted in 2021: 🟡. Beacon Hill Times, 2021-02-18 (tool): FHWA's 2009 MUTCD "mandated that all states establish a mile-based exit numbering system". WWLP (undated, about 2019) (tool): "Massachusetts, New Hampshire, and Delaware are the only three states that do not comply with the federal mandate". That list omits CT and NY, which were and are still partly sequential, so don't repeat it. The MassDOT project page returned 403.
- **Note:** The safest hook wording is "On most interstates…". Avoid "every state", "43 states" and the like. If the owner wants a surprise: "Connecticut is still renumbering I-95 in 2028" is ✅ attributed to CTDOT.

### C5. Subtracting exit numbers gives the distance, and where it breaks

- **Script (likely):** "Exit 150 to Exit 92? That's about 58 miles. No map needed."
- **Status:** 🟡, sourced in principle. The MUTCD Support text says mileage-based numbers help drivers in "determining their destination distances and travel mileage" (C1, ✅). Subtraction is the obvious way to do that, but no official source states "subtract the numbers". The **[arithmetic]** is mine.
- **Where it works:** two exits on the same interstate, in the same state, where both are mileage-based.
- **Where it breaks (each sourced):**
  1. **Across a state line.** The count restarts "within a State" (B1, ✅).
  2. **Overlaps.** Inside an overlap, exits carry the *other* route's numbers (C1 Par. 20, B2, ✅).
  3. **Rounding and suffix letters.** The number is the mile at the crossroad, rounded down "to the extent practical", and CTDOT may "round up or down" (C2, C4). So it's off by up to about a mile per exit **[inference]**.
  4. **Still-sequential routes** (C4, 🟡).
  5. **Realigned roads.** Caltrans: a postmile's numeric value "may not necessarily represent the odometer length from the beginning of the county or route" (C7, PDF). This is California-specific evidence that rebuilt sections aren't always re-measured. Don't generalize beyond "can drift a little".
  6. **Routes that leave and re-enter a state:** ❌. I found no source on how the count works (whether it resumes or restarts). Don't mention it.

### C6. Exit 0 is allowed

- **Script (likely):** Possible comment reply or fun aside: "And yes, there can be an Exit 0."
- **Status:** ✅ (PDF), as "allowed". Useful only as an aside.
- **Source:** MUTCD 11th Ed., Section 2E.22, Figures 2E-6 and 2E-7 footnote, p. 309–310 (as C1)
- **Quote (PDF):** "The freeway/freeway interchange where the beginning of the loop or spur route intersects with the mainline route may be called either Exit 1 or Exit 0 on the loop or spur route."
- **Note:** CTDOT has chosen "There is no Exit 0" (tool, C4). A real-world example (Kentucky's Natcher Parkway, a non-interstate) is from Wikipedia only.

### C7. California exception: postmiles reset at every county line (exit numbers are statewide)

- **Script (likely):** Only if needed to answer "my markers don't match!" comments. Not in the main story.
- **Status:** ✅ (PDF) for postmiles. ✅ (tool) for statewide exit numbers.
- **Source:** [Caltrans Postmile System](https://postmile.dot.ca.gov/PMQT/documents/CALTRANS%20POSTMILE%20SYSTEM%20061016.pdf), Caltrans Division of Transportation Planning, 2012-06-13 (accessed 2026-10-26)
- **Quote (PDF):** "Postmiles values are normally reset to zero at all county boundaries except when a route meanders between two counties." / "The numeric value contained within the postmile (34.576) may not necessarily represent the odometer length from the beginning of the county or route."
- **Source:** [Exit Numbering – CA Numbered Exit Uniform System](https://dot.ca.gov/programs/safety-programs/exit), Caltrans, undated (© 2026) (accessed 2026-10-26)
- **Quote (tool):** "Each exit number is determined by the number of miles it is from the beginning of the route." / "In 2002, Caltrans started numbering freeway exits in California."

## E. What a marker looks like (visual details)

### E1. Standard and intermediate markers

- **Script/visual:** Tall, narrow green sign with "MILE" on top and the digits **stacked vertically** (the D10-3 for 3 digits shows MILE / 1 / 5 / 0). The intermediate version adds a bottom band with ".3". When tenths are used, the whole-mile sign shows ".0".
- **Status:** ✅ (PDF).
- **Source:** MUTCD 11th Ed., Section 2H.11, Figures 2H-5/2H-6, Table 2H-1, Table 2E-5 (as B1, C1)
- **Quote (PDF), Option, Par. 04:** "Intermediate Reference Location (D10-1a through D10-3a) signs, which show the tenth of a mile with a decimal point, may be installed at one tenth of a mile, two tenths of a mile, or one-half mile intervals."
- **Quote (PDF), Standard, Par. 05:** "the reference location sign at the integer mile point shall display a decimal point and a zero numeral."
- **Quote (PDF), Standard, Par. 06:** "Reference Location and Intermediate Reference Location signs shall have a minimum mounting height of 4 feet"
- **Quote (PDF), Standard, Par. 12:** "reference location signs shall be installed on the right-hand side of the roadway." (The Option in Par. 13 allows the median where conditions limit.)
- **Size (PDF, Table 2H-1, freeway column):** D10-1 (1 digit) 12 x 24 in; D10-2 (2 digits) 12 x 36; D10-3 (3 digits) 12 x 48; D10-3a (with tenth) 12 x 60. Table 2E-5 H: "Numerals 10" (inches), "Words 4".
- **Colour (PDF, 2H.01 Standard Par. 09):** General Information signs (Chapter 2H, which includes reference location signs) "shall have white legends and borders on green rectangular-shaped backgrounds" (with listed exceptions that don't include D10 signs).

### E2. Enhanced markers (direction + shield + mile + tenth)

- **Script/visual:** "WEST / [US 12 shield] / MILE / 260 / .2" (MUTCD Figure 2H-7 example).
- **Status:** ✅ (PDF).
- **Source:** MUTCD 11th Ed., Section 2H.12, p. 467–468 (as B1)
- **Quote (PDF), Option, Par. 02:** "An Enhanced Reference Location (D10-4) sign, which enhances the reference location sign system by identifying the route, may be placed on freeways or expressways (instead of reference location signs) or on conventional roads."
- **Quote (PDF), Standard, Par. 05:** "they shall be vertical signs having a green background with a white legend and border, except for the route shield, which shall be the standard color and shape. The top line shall display the cardinal direction for the roadway. The second line shall display the applicable route shield for the roadway. The third line shall identify the mile reference for the location and the bottom line of the Intermediate Enhanced Reference Location sign shall give the tenth of a mile reference for the location preceded by a decimal point."
- **Note:** The brief asked whether the MUTCD says enhanced markers help "motorists report their location to emergency and service providers". **The 11th Edition 2H.12 contains no such sentence** (PDF, read in full). The purpose language is in 2H.11 Par. 02 (B1: "identifying the location of emergency incidents and traffic crashes") and 2E.22 Par. 02 (C1: "reporting their location in the event of an incident or breakdown"). Use those. The 2009 edition allowed "a blue or green background" for enhanced signs (tool, 2009 HTML), so older blue ones exist (WisDOT Dane County; TDOT snippet).

### E3. Exit signs: plaque, gore sign, left exits

- **Visual:** Green "EXIT 150" plaque on top of the guide sign. At the split, the gore sign reads "EXIT / 150 ↗". Left exits get a **yellow "LEFT"** tab above "EXIT". Suffix: "EXIT 150 A".
- **Status:** ✅ (PDF).
- **Source:** MUTCD 11th Ed., Sections 2E.09, 2E.22, 2E.26; Figures 2E-9, 2E-15 (as C1)
- **Quote (PDF), 2E.09 Standard:** "Guide signs on freeways and expressways, except as otherwise provided in this Manual, shall have white letters, symbols, arrows, and borders on a green background."
- **Quote (PDF), 2E.22 Standard, Par. 12:** "The word LEFT on the Left Exit Number plaque shall be a black legend on a yellow rectangular sign panel and shall be centered above the word EXIT."
- **Why green (only if asked):** MUTCD 1D.05 (PDF): "Green—indicated movements or actions permitted and direction guidance". That is the assigned *meaning*. No source gives a historical "why green". ❌ for any origin story.

## G. Myths (for hooks or comment replies)

### G1. Myth: one mile in five of the Interstate must be straight so planes can land

- **Status:** Debunked. 🟡 for the exact FHWA wording (quoted via a fact-check; the FHWA page itself returned 403 after redirecting to highways.dot.gov).
- **Source:** [Fact Check: US Interstate Highways Are NOT Required To Have Straight Sections…](https://leadstories.com/hoax-alert/2021/06/fact-check-us-interstate-highways-are-not-required-to-have-straight-sections.html), Christiana Dillard, Lead Stories, 2021-06-07 (accessed 2026-10-26), quoting FHWA "Interstate Highway System – The Myths" (https://www.fhwa.dot.gov/interstate/interstatemyths.cfm, now redirecting to https://highways.dot.gov/highway-history/interstate-system/50th-anniversary/interstate-highway-system-myths)
- **Quote (tool):** FHWA: "This myth is widespread on the Internet and in reference sources, but has no basis in law, regulation" (continues: "design manual--or fact.")
- **Note:** For a comment reply: "FHWA calls it a myth with no basis in law, regulation, design manual, or fact." FHWA also published "One Mile in Five: Debunking the Myth" (Public Roads, May/June 2000; snippet, not opened).

### G2. Myth: "Exit numbers just count the exits (Exit 12 = the 12th exit)"

- **Status:** ✅ that this is wrong almost everywhere: the MUTCD forbids it (C1, "The consecutive exit numbering method shall not be used"). It was true in the past and still is on a few Northeastern routes (C4, 🟡). **Best hook-myth** because it's what many viewers assume.

### G3. Myth: "Mile markers count from where you got on" / "they count down in the other direction"

- **Status:** ✅ wrong as stated. One count per route per state, from the south/west, with southbound/westbound markers "directly opposite" (B1). Driving south or west, the numbers simply go *down*. Good visual beat: the driver heading the "wrong" way watches them fall.

### G4. Myth: "911 can always find your phone, so the marker doesn't matter"

- **Status:** 🟡. Half true. The FCC rule (D3) requires 50 m accuracy for 80% of calls, so 20% may fall outside it, and rural crews still rely on mileposts (D2). Use it only as the honest caveat, not as a scare.

## Angles and hooks (suggested)

**Recommended: Angle 1, "The ruler you drive past"** (the owner's angle, tightened). Fact core: one count from the south/west, exits locked to it (C1), and the federal text naming both distance *and* breakdown reporting as the reasons (C1 Support).

- Hook A (owner's, hedged): "On most interstates, Exit 150 is about 150 miles from the state line." It's true for a driver heading north or east. Make the story driver head north or east, and if room add "counting from the south or west".
- Hook B: "Exit 92 isn't the 92nd exit. It's how far you've driven." (Hedge in the next line: "about, and in most states.")

**Angle 2, "The address with no street"**: lead with the night breakdown. Hook: "Break down at night on an empty interstate and there's no address. Except there is, on a green post every mile." Payoff: the count, then the subtraction trick. Keep the phone-location caveat (D3).

**Angle 3, "Count down to Exit 0"**: the myth G2 as the hook ("Exit numbers aren't counting exits"). Weaker emotionally, and the 911 payoff feels tacked on.

**Story details that make it visual:**

1. The marker up close: MILE over digits **stacked one above the other**, white on green, about a foot wide, at least 4 ft off the ground, on the right shoulder (E1). On tenth-mile stretches, a ".4" band underneath (E1, D1).
2. The opposite-side twin: the southbound marker stands **directly across** from the northbound one with the same number (B1). It's a strong overhead shot for the Section look: two posts, one count.
3. The exit plaque matching the marker: an "EXIT 150" plaque over the guide sign, then marker 150 just before the crossroad (C1, C2). Then the subtraction on screen: 150 − 92 = 58.
4. Night: hazards on, phone out, the driver reads "MILE 1 4 7 . 4" aloud. The dispatcher's map shows a rough phone circle (≈50 m) that snaps to the post (D3, D2). Keep the circle "approximate" and don't imply it's always wrong.
5. Optional: enhanced marker "NORTH / [I-shield] / MILE / 147 / .4" for the hero shot (E2).
