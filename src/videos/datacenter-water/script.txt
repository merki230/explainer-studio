## Where does a data center's water go?
> Short hook: One big data center can use up tens of millions of gallons of water a year. Where does it all go?
Towns across the US are pushing back on new data centers, and one of the biggest complaints is water.
A big data center can use up tens of millions of gallons a year.
So where does all that water go?
Most of it leaves through the roof, as vapor.
To see why, we'll follow the heat from one server rack on a hot afternoon, all the way up to that roof.
That heat starts with power, and these buildings use a lot more power than they did a few years ago.
A data center used to need a few megawatts, and Virginia's state auditors say a typical one now needs more than a hundred.
Inside, the average server rack still draws under 8 kilowatts, an industry survey finds.
But some racks of today's AI chips draw around 120 kilowatts.
Nearly all that electricity turns into heat, so more power means more heat to get rid of.
At many data centers, including this one, water carries that heat away.
Look at the back of this rack, where the fans blow the heat out as hot air.
The hot air passes over coils of cold water, and the water carries the heat out of the room.
Pipes take the warmed water up to a cooling tower on the roof.
Inside the tower, the water rains down while big fans pull outside air through.
On each trip down, a small part of the water evaporates, and as it does, it takes heat away from the water that's left.
Each gallon that evaporates carries off about as much heat as a space heater puts out in an hour and 40 minutes.
Warm that gallon by 10 degrees instead, and it carries off only about a hundredth as much.
That's why towers let the water evaporate.
Data centers use the most water in hot weather, the Pacific Institute says, so this afternoon the towers are working their hardest.
Over a whole year, a typical site run by a big tech company in the institute's study pulled in about 44 million gallons.
That's about what 400 American families use at home.
And most of the water those sites pulled in was drinking water.
Back in the tower, only part of that water evaporates, and the rest drops back down for another trip around the loop.
So why can't the tower keep sending that same water around forever?

## Why don't they just reuse the water?
> Short hook: A data center's cooling tower reuses its water several times, so why does it still drain some away?
The cooling tower on this data center's roof already reuses its water, sending the same water around the loop several times.
Every time some of that water evaporates, only the water itself leaves, and the calcium, magnesium and silica stay in the loop.
The loop water holds more and more minerals, and engineers measure that in what they call cycles of concentration.
Many towers run at two to four cycles, the EPA says, which means up to four times the minerals of the fresh water.
If the cycles go too high, the minerals form scale, like the crust inside a kettle.
The minerals also speed up corrosion in the pipes.
And the warm, wet tower is an easy place for bacteria to grow, so pumps add chemicals to the loop.
To keep the minerals in check, a valve drains off some of the loop water, which engineers call blowdown.
Fresh water replaces what's drained and what evaporated, so the tower keeps drawing water all afternoon.
In all, at four cycles, about three quarters of the water coming in leaves as vapor, and the other quarter is drained.
Going from three cycles to six cuts the drained water by about half, the EPA says, but some always has to go.
That drained water carries the minerals and chemicals, so why not let it run into the nearest river?

## Why not release it into a river?
> Short hook: When a data center drains water from its cooling towers, why not just pipe it into the nearest river?
At a data center like this one, most of the cooling water has already gone up from the roof as vapor.
What's left is the drained water, and it usually goes down the sewer to the city's wastewater plant, under local rules.
Piping that water straight into a river takes a federal permit, with limits on what the water carries.
When Ohio proposed a permit for data centers, the draft set limits on the water's dissolved minerals, its chlorine and its acidity.
The Clean Water Act behind those permits even lists heat as a pollutant, next to things like sand and rock.
Instead of a sewer or a river, one town in Washington State found a third place for the drained water.
In Quincy, the city cleans the drained water from a Microsoft data center and pipes it back for the towers to use again.
The minerals the city removes end up as solid salt in lined ponds, instead of in a river.
Microsoft agreed to cover the system's $31 million cost.
The EPA's case study puts the water saving at an estimated 138 million gallons a year.
Even in Quincy, the towers still send water into the air.
Could a data center be cooled without evaporating any water at all?

## Why not cool it without water?
> Short hook: A data center can be cooled with no cooling water at all, so why doesn't every data center do that?
This data center could skip evaporation by swapping its towers for air-cooled machines.
They work like a car's radiator, with fans and no water.
Microsoft's newest design works this way, filling its cooling loop once during construction and circulating that water from then on.
The company says each data center built this way will avoid more than 125 million liters of water a year, about 33 million gallons.
But while air-cooled machines use no cooling water, they use more energy, Berkeley Lab says.
For its new design, Microsoft says the extra energy is nominal, meaning small.
Either way, that extra electricity still has to be made, and making electricity uses water too.
Across the US in 2023, Berkeley Lab found, making data centers' electricity took about 12 times the water they used up on site.
Back on the roof this afternoon, most of the water goes up as vapor, and the rest usually goes down the city drain.
This data center can keep that water out of the air, but only by using more electricity.
And part of the water this data center saves can then get used up at a power plant instead.
The next time your town argues about a data center's water, ask about its electricity too.
