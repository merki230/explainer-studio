Angle (owner-approved): you drift toward the edge of the highway and the car suddenly roars and shakes. Why do those grooves make that loud, specific noise, and why are they there? Grooves are milled into the shoulder, edge line or center line at a set spacing and depth. At highway speed each tire drops into them dozens of times a second. That makes a loud hum inside the car and a shake in the steering wheel and seat, meant to alert a drowsy or distracted driver. Speed and spacing set the pitch, and musical roads use that to play tunes. Agencies credit the strips with fewer run-off-road and head-on crashes.

Owner's hook: "Drift toward the edge of the highway, and the road itself starts yelling at you." Question: "So how does a road make that noise?"

## A. What they are

### A1. Rumble strips are grooves milled (or raised elements) along the shoulder, edge line or center line, meant to alert drivers by sound and vibration that they've left the lane

- **Script (likely):** "Look at the shoulder. Just past the white line, there's a row of shallow grooves cut into the pavement."
- **Status:** ✅ verified (researcher).
- **Source:** [Longitudinal Rumble Strips and Stripes (Proven Safety Countermeasures fact sheet, FHWA-SA-21-036)](https://www.sjtpo.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/05/fhwa-pscm-longitudinal-rumble-strips.pdf), FHWA Office of Safety, PDF metadata dated 2023-03 (hosted by the South Jersey Transportation Planning Organization; FHWA's own copy at highways.fhwa.dot.gov returns 403) (accessed 2026-10-18)
- **Quote:** "Longitudinal rumble strips are milled or raised elements on the pavement intended to alert drivers through vibration and sound that their vehicle has left the travel lane. They can be installed on the shoulder, edge line, or at or near the center line of an undivided roadway." / "Rumble stripes are edge line or center line rumble strips where the pavement marking is placed over the rumble strip."
- **Source:** [State of the Practice for Shoulder and Center Line Rumble Strip Implementation on Non-Freeway Facilities (FHWA-HRT-17-026), Chapter 1](https://www.fhwa.dot.gov/publications/research/safety/17026/002.cfm), FHWA, 2017-03 (accessed 2026-10-18)
- **Quote (tool):** "Milled rumble strips, which are most prevalent, are milled into the roadway surface using a rotary milling machine." / "Raised rumble strips include side-by-side raised pavement markers, rumble bars, or plastic inserts"
- **Source:** [Guidelines for Installing Rumble Strips on California State Highways (attachment to Traffic Safety Bulletin 20-07)](https://dot.ca.gov/-/media/dot-media/programs/safety-programs/documents/policy/20-07-rumble-strips-guidelines-and-memo-011421-a11y.pdf), Caltrans, 2021-01-14 (accessed 2026-10-18)
- **Quote:** "A rumble strip is a series of milled indentations on the centerline or along paved shoulders to alert or warn drivers they are leaving their lane of travel." / "projects on the State Highway System shall install rumble strips when any of the following conditions are met: 1. on all freeway and expressway shoulders"
- **Source (rolled vs milled):** [Rumble Strips: Frequently Asked Questions](https://dot.alaska.gov/stwddes/dcstraffic/rumble/rumble_faqs.shtml), Alaska DOT&PF, undated (policy file named 2009) (accessed 2026-10-18)
- **Quote (tool):** "Rumble strips are grooved patterns rolled or milled into the pavement perpendicular to the direction of travel." / "A study by the Virginia highway department showed milled-in rumble strips produce a vibration level 12 times greater" (the tool cut this sentence short; re-quote before use)
- **Note:** Types: **milled** (cut by a rotating drum, the common kind and the one to show), **rolled** (pressed into fresh asphalt, older, weaker), **raised** (bumps or markers stuck on top), and **sinusoidal** ("mumble strips", a wavy milled profile, E1). Caltrans requires them on every freeway and expressway shoulder in California. That is one state's rule, not a national one. Don't say "every highway has them."

### A2. A typical groove is about 12 to 16 inches long across the shoulder, 7 inches along the road, half an inch deep, and spaced 12 inches center to center

- **Script (likely):** "Each groove is about half an inch deep, and they're spaced a foot apart."
- **Status:** ✅ verified (fact-checker re-opened FHWA-HRT-17-026 Ch.3: "the typical SRS designs are 12 or 16 inches long, 7 inches wide, 0.5 inches deep, and spaced 12 inches apart") as **typical practice from a survey of state non-freeway practice**, not a federal standard. Spoken as "about" / "typically" / "usually".
- **Source:** [FHWA-HRT-17-026, Chapter 3 (state of the practice)](https://www.fhwa.dot.gov/publications/research/safety/17026/004.cfm), FHWA, 2017-03 (accessed 2026-10-18)
- **Quote (tool):** "the typical SRS designs are 12 or 16 inches long, 7 inches wide, 0.5 inches deep, and spaced 12 inches apart." / "The majority of departments specify 12-inch center-to-center CLRS spacing" / "Most commonly, 0.5 inch is specified for rumble strip depth"
- **Source (definitions):** FHWA-HRT-17-026, Chapter 1, as in A1
- **Quote (tool):** Length: "Dimension of the strip that is perpendicular to the travel directions of the roadway." / Width: "Dimension of the strip that is parallel to the travel direction of the roadway." / Spacing: "It is most often measured from the center of the strip to the center of the adjacent strip."
- **Source (one state's standard):** Caltrans guidelines, as in A1
- **Quote:** "The bicycle community raised concerns regarding milled rumble strips (½ inch to ⅝ inch in depth) in the mid to late 1990's." / Figure 7 (edge line rumble strip) labels "5"", "6"" and "12"" (groove length along the road, stripe width, center-to-center spacing).
- **Source (measured examples):** Oregon DOT report OR-RD-19-10 (C2 below), Figure 3.1 reproducing WSDOT data (Sexton 2014): nine Washington roads with depths 0.375 to 0.5 in, widths 6.0 to 6.9 in, lengths 8 to 12 in, spacing 12 in on seven of the nine (18 and 24 in on the other two).
- **Note:** **Focus object for realism.** FHWA's "length" is across the road and "width" is along it, which is the opposite of everyday usage. In plain words for the scene: each groove is a scoop about 1 to 1¼ ft across the shoulder and about 7 in long in the direction of travel. The bottom is curved (cylindrical, cut by a drum), about ½ in deep at the middle. There is a flat ridge of pavement about 5 in between scoops (12 in center to center). A row runs continuously along the shoulder, just outside the white edge line. Caltrans' figure puts it about 6 in plus or minus 2 in from the stripe. Texture: milled grooves show a coarser, freshly exposed aggregate face than the surrounding asphalt.

## B. How the noise and the shake are made

### B1. The tire drops into each groove; the drop squeezes the tire and the air trapped in the groove, which makes noise and vibration

- **Script (likely):** "Every time your tire drops into a groove and climbs out, it squeezes the air trapped underneath and jolts the wheel."
- **Status:** 🟡 plausible (fact-checker): Oregon PDF text could not be opened by me, so the "squeezes the tire and the trapped air" quote is researcher-read only; NCHRP 1107 "abrupt transitions" is the only part I could not re-open either.
- **Source:** [Rumble Strip Design Evaluation Based on Exterior Noise Using Finite Element Analysis (OR-RD-19-10)](https://www.oregon.gov/ODOT/Programs/ResearchDocuments/RumbleStripsFinal.pdf), Paris Kalathas, Christopher Parrish, Yue Zhang (Oregon State University) for Oregon DOT, 2019-04 (accessed 2026-10-18)
- **Quote:** "The cylindrical design functionality is based on the tire falling into the grooves. The fall creates pressure to the tire and to the air in the groove's cavity between the tire and the pavement, resulting in noise and vibrations." / "Rumble strips produce audible and tactile signals to alert drivers of lane departures"
- **Source:** [Effective Low-Noise Rumble Strips (NCHRP Research Report 1107), Chapter 2](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/27861/chapter/4), Paul R. Donavan, Carrie J. Janello, Judith Rochat, Shannon McKenna, National Academies Press, 2024 (accessed 2026-10-18)
- **Quote (tool):** "These strips have abrupt transitions as a tire enters and exits the indentions."
- **Note:** This is the mechanism to show: tire into the groove, tire out, many times a second. The script's "the road is yelling" is a figure of speech. Physically, the sound comes from the tire, the trapped air and the car body. The road sets the rhythm.

### B2. The hum's pitch is how many grooves the tire hits per second: at 60 mph, grooves 12 inches apart give about 88 Hz

- **Script (likely):** "At highway speed your tires hit nearly a hundred grooves a second, and that steady drumming is the hum you hear."
- **Status:** ✅ verified (fact-checker re-opened NCHRP 1107 Ch.2 and quote matches; 60 mph = 88 ft/s ÷ 1 ft = 88 per second) for **"88 Hz at 60 mph with 12-inch spacing"**. 🟡 **derived** for any other speed (our arithmetic, below). "Nearly a hundred a second" is 🟡 derived; say "dozens of times a second" for ✅ wording, or attribute the 60 mph figure.
- **Source:** NCHRP Research Report 1107, Chapter 2, as in B1
- **Quote (tool):** "The 12 in. spacing of the cylindrical "bump" grind design produces a repetition rate of 88 Hz at 60 mph." / (sinusoidal design) "At 60 mph, this spacing corresponds to 75 Hertz (Hz)," (14-inch wavelength)
- **Derived (our arithmetic, not a quote):** hits per second = speed ÷ spacing. 1 mph = 1.4667 ft/s, so with 12-inch (1 ft) spacing the hits per second equal the speed in ft/s.
  - 55 mph → 80.7 per second (≈ 81 Hz)
  - 60 mph → 88.0 per second (88 Hz; matches NCHRP 1107)
  - **65 mph → 95.3 per second (≈ 95 Hz)**
  - 70 mph → 102.7 per second (≈ 103 Hz)
  - 75 mph → 110.0 per second (110 Hz)
  - For music fans (derived, 🟡): 88 Hz is close to the note F2 (87.3 Hz), about an octave and a half below middle C. A low hum.
- **Note:** At a shallow drift angle the spacing along the tire's path is still about 12 in (the angle changes it by well under 1%). Each tire hits the grooves on its own, so the front and rear tires on that side both hum. Say "about a hundred a second" only with the 🟡 flag. The ✅ line is: "At 60 miles an hour, the grooves hit your tire about 88 times a second."

### B3. Faster speed or closer grooves raise the pitch; spacing sets the note (the principle behind musical roads)

- **Script (likely):** "Drive faster, or space the grooves closer, and the hum gets higher."
- **Status:** ✅ verified (researcher) for the physics: two independent sources give f = v / spacing. 🟡 for "you'd hear the rumble strip's pitch change with speed", since no rumble-strip source states it in those words; it follows from B2's formula.
- **Source:** [Honda Needs a Tune-Up](https://davidsd.org/2008/12/honda-needs-a-tune-up), David Simmons-Duffin (physicist; personal blog), 2008-12-23 (accessed 2026-10-18)
- **Quote (tool):** "The vibration frequency is f = v/d, where v is the car's speed, and d is the distance over which the road pattern repeats." / "The width that really matters is the total width of the spacing plus groove (s+g)."
- **Source:** [Driving Rhythms of the Asphaltophone: Musical Roads](https://hearingreview.com/practice-building/practice-management/continuing-education/driving-rhythms-asphaltophone-musical-road), Marshall Chasin, AuD, Hearing Review, 2014-06-05 (accessed 2026-10-18)
- **Quote (tool):** "The distance spacing defined the pitch and the depth defined the sound level." / "where f is frequency (262 Hz or 262/second) and v is the rate that the car passes by each groove (100 km/h)." / "So, for a middle C (262 Hz) to be heard, each groove in the road needs to be about 4 inches or 10 cm apart" (sentence continues; truncated by the tool)
- **Note:** A blog and a trade-magazine column, both by subject experts, agree with the NCHRP 1107 figure (B2), which supports the formula. Line to use: "Spacing sets the pitch, depth sets the loudness" (Chasin), attributed or paraphrased.

### B4. Inside the car, the strips are designed to be clearly louder than normal road noise

- **Script (likely):** Probably not spoken as a number. If it is: "Engineers aim for a jump in cabin noise you can't miss."
- **Status:** ✅ verified (researcher) for FHWA's "minimum 3- to 5-dB change" (tool-quoted; re-quote). 🟡 for NCHRP 641's 6–12 dBA (rural) and 10–15 dBA (freeway) targets: I only saw them as quoted by the Oregon report, not in NCHRP 641 itself.
- **Source:** [FHWA-HRT-17-026, Chapter 2](https://www.fhwa.dot.gov/publications/research/safety/17026/003.cfm), FHWA, 2017-03 (accessed 2026-10-18)
- **Quote (tool):** "rumble strips should provide a minimum 3- to 5-dB change in noise level over the noise inside the vehicle." / "a 4-dB or greater change in sound level was considered to be sufficient to alert drivers who were awake." / rumble strips "must provide an audible level of noise that is noticeably greater than the ambient background noise"
- **Source (secondary):** Oregon DOT report OR-RD-19-10, as in B1, §3.1 summarising NCHRP Report 641 (2009)
- **Quote:** "The threshold ranges for the interior SLD are given for rural roads to be 6 – 12 dBA and for freeways to be 10 – 15 dBA, but the report does not suggest any threshold ranges for vibration levels."
- **Source (honest gap):** FHWA-HRT-17-026, as above
- **Quote (tool):** "no research has been conducted to determine whether these assumptions are valid." (about the minimum sound needed to alert a drowsy driver)
- **Note:** Don't speak a dB figure; viewers can't picture it. If one is needed, use the FHWA minimum with attribution. Measured examples (Terhaar and Braslau 2015, MnDOT, as charted in OR-RD-19-10 Fig. 3.3): interior levels with the strip around 75–86 dBA vs about 60–72 dBA with no strip, depending on vehicle and speed. That's a chart reading, not a quote, so don't use it.

## C. Why they're there, and what they're meant to do

### C1. They're aimed at drowsy, distracted or inattentive drivers who drift out of the lane

- **Script (likely):** "They're there for the driver who's nodding off or looking at a phone and drifting toward the edge."
- **Status:** ✅ verified (researcher). "Looking at a phone" is 🎭 illustrative of FHWA's "distracted".
- **Source:** FHWA-SA-21-036, as in A1
- **Quote:** "rumble strips and stripes are designed to address these crashes by alerting distracted, drowsy, or otherwise inattentive drivers who drift from their lane."
- **Source:** Caltrans Traffic Safety Bulletin 20-07, as in A1
- **Quote:** "Why it works: Rumble strips alert inattentive or drowsy drivers that their vehicle is drifting out of their lane onto the shoulder or into the opposing lane."

### C2. The warning is meant to be sound plus a mild shake, so the driver corrects course

- **Script (likely):** "It's built to startle you, not to steer you: the grooves are only about half an inch deep."
- **Status:** 🟡 plausible. Caltrans says "mild tactile warning to correct their steering path"; the depth is ✅ (A2). "Not to steer you" is our wording, not a source's. ✅ alternative: "The grooves are only about half an inch deep: enough to roar and shake, and Caltrans calls the shake a mild warning to correct your steering."
- **Source:** Caltrans guidelines, as in A1
- **Quote:** "As a vehicle's tires pass over a rumble strip, the driver receives an auditory and mild tactile warning to correct their steering path."
- **Source (design caution, transverse strips):** MUTCD Section 6M.06 as reproduced in NCUTCD 24B-TTC-06 (G1)
- **Quote:** "transverse rumble strips should be designed in a manner that does not promote unnecessary braking or erratic steering maneuvers by road users."
- **Note:** The brief's "without throwing the car off course" has no direct source for **shoulder** strips. The MUTCD line above is about transverse strips across the lane. Keep the script line to "a warning" (✅) or the Caltrans wording.

## D. Do they work?

### D1. Road departures are more than half of the fatal crashes in the US each year

- **Script (likely):** "In the US, more than half of the deaths on the road start with a car leaving its lane." (Check wording: FHWA says "fatal roadway crashes", i.e. crashes, not deaths.)
- **Status:** ✅ verified (researcher) as "more than half of fatal crashes". "Deaths" is 🟡; use "fatal crashes".
- **Source:** FHWA-SA-21-036, as in A1
- **Quote:** "With roadway departure crashes accounting for more than half of the fatal roadway crashes annually in the United States"
- **Note:** A roadway departure covers leaving the lane to either side: off the road or across the center line. FHWA gives no year or count here; for a number, look up FHWA's current roadway-departure page (403 for us).

### D2. Freeways (the video's setting): shoulder rumble strips cut single-vehicle run-off-road crashes by roughly 11 to 21 percent in the studies FHWA cites

- **Script (likely):** "On rural freeways, studies in two states found about a fifth fewer cars running off the road after the grooves went in."

## Myths and what the evidence says

| Myth | What the evidence says | Entry |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "They're random bumps to rattle you." | They're precisely spaced grooves, typically a foot apart and half an inch deep, cut by a milling drum. The spacing is what makes the steady hum. | A2, B2 |
| "The noise is the road scraping your tires." | The tire drops into each groove and climbs out dozens of times a second; the drops squeeze the tire and the trapped air. The pitch is just the hit rate. | B1, B2 |
| "They're raised bumps." | Most are milled grooves cut into the pavement. Raised versions exist but are less common; rolled-in ones shake far less. | A1 |
| "It's the same everywhere." | Pitch changes with speed and spacing. At 60 mph, a foot apart, about 88 hits a second; faster means higher. | B2, B3 |
| "They're mostly about cars hitting the guardrail." | Also head-on crashes: center-line strips target drivers drifting into oncoming traffic. FHWA's biggest figure is for those. | D3 |
| "They fill with snow and stop working." | FHWA: studies show no evidence of snow, ice or rain build-up problems. | E3 |
| "Everyone nearby hears them all night." | They're hit only when a car drifts, and on busy roads normal traffic noise dominates, per Caltrans. But complaints do happen near homes, hence "mumble strips". | E1 |
| "There must be a warning sign for them." | The MUTCD has no rumble strip warning sign; a proposal (2025) isn't adopted yet. | G1 |
| "The Lancaster musical road is off-key because people speed." | The city says the notes are off "possibly due to miscalculations"; one physicist's analysis blames the groove spacing itself. | F4 |

## Hook facts (surprising, concrete)

1. **At 60 mph your tire drops into a groove about 88 times a second.** That steady drumming is the hum (✅ NCHRP 1107, tool-quoted; 🟡 for "about 95 at 65").
2. **Spacing sets the pitch, so roads can play songs.** Lancaster, California's road plays the William Tell Overture. The first one kept neighbors awake and was moved out of town (✅ F3), and it's famously off-key (✅ city's hedge, F4).
3. **Federal engineers say each dollar of grooves returns benefits over a hundred times the cost** (✅ as "benefit/cost ratios that exceed 100", D4).
4. Optional: **there's no official sign warning you about them**; the road just starts making noise (✅ G1).
