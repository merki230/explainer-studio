## Description (YouTube)

Why don't US traffic lights count down for drivers?

In China, a map app can count down a red light, and its maker says it doesn't need to be wired to the light.

Do the lights near you wait for your car, or run on a timer?

Illustrative: the two intersections, the cars and the people in them are drawn examples, not real places or real drivers; the Chinese-style corner and the US corner are the same drawn street dressed two ways. The map app on the dash phone is a generic drawing, not any real app's screen. The orange dots over the cars, the floating timeline and the orange marks on it are drawings of data, made to show the idea described in the method's patent; they are not the app's actual system. The time-lapse of many days is a drawing; the cycle length is invented, and so are the red and green times. The countdown numbers on the lights and on the phone are examples, not measurements. The two cars approaching the yellow, one stopping and one going through, are staged for the picture, and the dashed 3-2-1 at the US light shows what a countdown would look like; it is not a real display. The Lab scene with 10,000 cells stands for the preprint's test intersections; it is not a map of real ones. The in-pavement loop and the pole camera are both shown at one corner so the picture can show both kinds of sensor; real corners usually have one kind or the other, and the controller cabinet is a generic drawing.

A US signal manual says no numbers go on the lights drivers watch, and it also says research found warnings before the yellow increase crashes; that is the manual's statement, and the studies of countdowns themselves disagree. Many US lights are actuated, meaning detectors call and extend the green, and one Oregon State researcher says an actuated light often changes only one to four seconds after the decision to change it. Countdowns, on the light or in an app, work best on lights that keep a steady schedule. The 75 percent figure is one research team's preprint, not peer reviewed, and tested estimating light timing, not the countdown drivers see.

(How the video's wording maps to these. "In China, many traffic lights count down for drivers" is the 2023 review's "widely used in China". "In the US, that's not allowed" and "US rules keep countdowns off the lights drivers watch" are MUTCD 4D.06 ¶02 and 4D.26 ¶16; the 11th Edition (2023) carries the same sentences in Sections 4E.01 and 4F.17, but those section numbers were read only on an unofficial mirror, so they are not cited above. "The rulebook says research found warnings before the yellow increase crashes" is 4D.26 ¶17, in the manual's words; the manual does not cite which research, and the 2023 review found no reviewed countdown study that used crash counts, which is why "studies of countdowns disagree". "Road sensors" covers in-road and over-road detectors (Detector Handbook Vol. I), and "decide when the green ends" rests on the Timing Manual's phases "called and extended in response to vehicle detectors"; a green can also end by reaching its maximum time, so "decide" is fair, not exclusive. "Often just seconds before the light changes" is one Oregon State researcher's statement, "typically only one to four seconds", in a 2017 university release. "The app's maker says" the countdown needs no connection to the light is the company's own statement in the Jimu and TMTPost pieces; the company also runs pilots with traffic police in some cities, and neither it nor its competitor says what share of lights is which, so the video says "without needing a connection" and "can". "The app sees that moment on the phones of drivers using it" and "the starts repeat with the same gap, and that gap is the light's cycle" are the Sina 2024 description and the patent's period in the start-time offsets, with the BMW patent's "integer multiple of the cycle time"; the patent is a patent, not a description of the shipped product. "One research team ... about 10,000 intersections ... more than three in four timing estimates were within five seconds" is the arXiv abstract's "more than 75% of estimations demonstrating less than five seconds of error"; the abstract does not name the metric, and the team is a different one using a similar method. "Work best on lights that keep a steady schedule" is the Oregon State researcher's "work well at fixed-time signals" and "may not be practical for actuated signals", plus the Nanjing University expert's statement that fixed timing is easier to estimate and more accurate. "Many US lights ... wait for your car" is the FHWA description of actuated control; no national share is given and the video does not give one.)
