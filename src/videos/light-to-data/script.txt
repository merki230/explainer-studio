It starts in a data center as 1s and 0s called bits, often stored on a server's flash drives. When your phone asks for it, software, often helped by the network card, cuts it into numbered packets, each addressed like an envelope. They leave as electrical signals and move onto fiber through small plug-in laser modules. In many modules, a chip first cleans up the signal, then a driver turns each bit into a pulse of current. The laser is a tiny crystal that glows when current flows through it, and its mirror-smooth ends bounce the light back and forth until it builds into a beam. Many fast modules in data centers use four brightness levels, so each flash carries two bits. A tiny lens usually focuses the beam into the fiber's core, a glass channel about nine thousandths of a millimeter wide, inside a strand about as thin as a hair. The light is infrared, because that's where glass is clearest, so the colors you see here are ours. Over distance it fades and blurs, so each link reaches only a few hundred meters to tens of kilometers, and routers along the way read it back into bits. Inside a router's module, a light sensor turns the flashes into a trickle of current. Two amplifiers turn that trickle into clean electrical steps, and a chip decides whether each one is a 1 or a 0. Another chip reads each packet's destination address and finds the closest match in its table of routes, at up to hundreds of billions of lookups a second. Two amplifiers clean up that current, and a chip checks it once per bit, reading a 1 above a set level and a 0 below. The flashes arrive as one unbroken stream, like Morse code with no pauses.
