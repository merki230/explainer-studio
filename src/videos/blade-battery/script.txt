BYD drove a nail through two battery cells. One burst into flames, and the other didn't even smoke.
You might think every EV battery has the same fire risk.
In BYD's own test, a nickel cell went over 900 degrees Fahrenheit, and BYD's iron-phosphate Blade stayed at 140 or below.
So why does chemistry matter that much?
When a charged nickel cell overheats, it can release oxygen that helps fuel the fire.
Iron phosphate holds its oxygen much more tightly.
BYD says the Blade's long, flat shape also helps the cell cool.
Each cell is more than three feet long and just over half an inch thick.
Iron-phosphate cells can still overheat out of control, but it takes a higher temperature, and they give off less heat.
Tesla said in 2021 it was moving its standard-range cars to iron phosphate as well.
That chemistry stores less energy for its weight, though.
Tesla's 4680 nickel cell holds about fifty percent more than the Blade, German researchers found.
