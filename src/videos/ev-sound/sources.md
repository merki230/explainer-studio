# Sources: ev-sound (No. 007)

Researcher draft, 2026-10-22. Written before the script exists, so each entry gives a *likely* line. The fact-checker sets the final statuses; where a later fact-check table differs from an entry, the table wins.

Agreed angle (owner): a speaker hidden in the front of most electric cars (an Acoustic Vehicle Alerting System, AVAS) plays a designed, synthetic sound at low speed. It exists because pedestrians can't hear quiet EVs at low speed, especially people who are blind or have low vision. Payoff: next time an EV pulls away slowly, listen.

Owner's hook: "There's a speaker hidden in the front of most electric cars, and it's not for music." **Verdict: holds as worded** (A1, A2, A3). A stronger, rule-backed companion line also holds: "Every new electric car sold in the US has to make a sound at low speed." (B1, B5; see the scope note on "every").

Everything was accessed on 2026-10-22. Quotes are verbatim. A long quote is given in pieces joined by "…" or "/".

## A. The hidden speaker

### A1. Most cars carry one AVAS speaker at the front bumper, optionally a second at the back

- **Script (likely):** "There's a speaker hidden in the front of most electric cars, and it's not for music."
- **Status:** ✅ verified (researcher, read directly) **as an industry statement**. Scope: Siemens, an acoustic-engineering software vendor, describing vehicles "today" (2020), worldwide, electric and hybrid. It's not a census. Corroborated by one maker's own description (A2), a supplier (A2 note) and the US rule's front microphone (A4).
- **Source:** [Acoustic vehicle alerting system: Designing the right exterior sound and assessing its performance by integrating simulation and testing](https://www.plm.automation.siemens.com/media/global/en/SiemensSW-Acoustic-vehicle-alerting-system_WhitePaper_tcm27-76714.pdf), Siemens Digital Industries Software (white paper), 2020-04 (accessed 2026-10-22)
- **Quote:** "Although most vehicles today are equipped with one speaker on the front bumper and optionally one in the back, to enable sound creation with directivity, multiple speaker configurations with control would be preferred."
- **Note:**
  - "Hidden": the speaker sits on or behind the bumper cover, out of sight. One supplier's design even uses the grille cover itself as the speaker cone (A2 note).
  - Siemens also mentions speakers "in the motor compartment under the hood" for some designs. So "in the front" covers both; don't say "in the bumper" for every car.
  - The white paper sells Siemens software. Fine as an industry description, not spoken as a citation.

### A2. One maker: the speaker is in the front bumper, loud to pedestrians, nearly silent inside

- **Script (likely):** "It's aimed outward, at people on the sidewalk. Inside, the driver barely hears it."
- **Status:**
  - ✅ (tool-quoted; re-quote) for one maker's own wording, quoted by trade press.
  - 🟡 as a general claim. Scope: Honda's 2020 CR-V Hybrid (US).
  - Honda's own newsroom page was not opened. NHTSA counts cabin intrusion as a cost drivers may notice (GAO summary, search snippet only), so "barely" is right and "can't hear" is not.
- **Source:** [Honda: 2020 CR-V gets ADAS suite standard, hybrid version with speaker in bumper](https://www.repairerdrivennews.com/?p=40202), John Huetter, Repairer Driven News, 2019-09-30 (accessed 2026-10-22)
- **Quote (tool):** "A speaker mounted in the front bumper generates a distinct audible alert that varies in volume depending on vehicle speed" / "and is easily noticeable to pedestrians, but nearly inaudible to the driver or passengers under most circumstances."
- **Supporting (supplier):** [Finding a Purpose for an EV Grille](https://gardnerweb.com/news/finding-a-purpose-for-an-ev-grille), GW Editorial Team, GardnerWeb (Gardner Business Media), 2020-05-12 (accessed 2026-10-22). Quote (tool): "a modified speaker is integrated into the grille" / "the cover itself acts as a diaphragm to transmit and amplify the noise outward" / it "could be installed behind the front bumper" (hybrids with a traditional grille). Supplier: Hyundai Mobis.
- **Supporting (maker manual):** Nissan LEAF 2023 manual (E2): to check the system, "check that the sound can be heard from the front" (tool paraphrase of the troubleshooting step; re-quote).
- **Note:** **Company rule.** Don't name Honda, Hyundai Mobis or Nissan in the voice. The 2020 Repairer Driven News article ([NHTSA delays date for hybrids, EVs to meet noise rule with tech like bumper speakers](https://www.repairerdrivennews.com/2020/08/28/nhtsa-delays-date-for-hybrids-evs-to-meet-noise-rule-with-tech-like-bumper-speakers/), 2020-08-28) repeats the Honda quote. It adds that the speaker matters to collision repairers because it sits among parts they handle. That fits "hidden".

### A3. US regulators assume every light-duty EV has a speaker system

- **Script (likely):** Not spoken directly. It backs "most" (even "nearly all") for the US.
- **Status:** ✅ verified (researcher, tool-quoted; re-quote). It's NHTSA's modelling assumption in the 2016 final rule, not a survey.
- **Source:** [Federal Motor Vehicle Safety Standards; Minimum Sound Requirements for Hybrid and Electric Vehicles, final rule, 81 FR 90416 (FR Doc. 2016-28804)](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2016-12-14/html/2016-28804.htm), NHTSA, 2016-12-14 (accessed 2026-10-22)
- **Quote (tool):** footnote 4: "light vehicle EVs are all assumed to be equipped with speaker systems" / cost section: "adding a speaker system in order to comply with the requirements of this rule."
- **Note:** The rule itself demands a **sound**, not a speaker. An EV could in theory comply some other way. That's why the hook says "most" and not "every".

### A4. The US test requires the stopped car's sound to reach a microphone 2 m straight ahead

- **Script (likely):** Optional reason for "front": "The test puts a microphone right in front of the car, where you'd be crossing."
- **Status:** ✅ verified (tool-quoted; re-quote) for the rule text. 🟡 as the **reason** makers put speakers in front: that's an inference, and no source says it in those words.
- **Source:** [49 CFR § 571.141, Standard No. 141; Minimum sound requirements for hybrid and electric vehicles](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/49/571.141), Cornell Law School LII (current CFR text), undated (accessed 2026-10-22)
- **Quote (tool):** S5.1.1.2: "For directivity, the vehicle must emit a sound measured at the microphone on the line CC'" / S6.5: front-center microphone "placed on the line CC', 2m forward of the line PP' at a height of 1.2m above ground level." / S4: "Front plane of the vehicle means a vertical plane tangent to the leading edge of the vehicle during forward operation."
- **Note:** Line CC' is the car's centerline and PP' is its front plane, so this microphone sits 2 m straight ahead of the front bumper. Visual gift: show the test microphone on a stand in front of a stopped car (The Lab).

## B. The US rule (FMVSS 141)

### B1. Who must make the sound: every EV, and every hybrid that can move on electric power alone, up to 10,000 lb GVWR

- **Script (likely):** "In the US, every new electric car has to make a sound at low speed."
- **Status:** ✅ verified (tool-quoted; re-quote).
- **Source:** 49 CFR § 571.141 (Cornell LII), as in A4
- **Quote (tool):** S3: "(a) Electric vehicles with a gross vehicle weight rating (GVWR) of 4,536 Kg or less that are passenger cars, multipurpose passenger vehicles, trucks, or buses;" / "(b) Hybrid vehicles with a gross vehicle weight rating (GVWR) of 4,536 Kg or less…" / S4: "Hybrid vehicle means a motor vehicle which has more than one means of propulsion for which the vehicle's propulsion system can propel the vehicle in the normal travel mode in at least one forward drive gear or reverse without the internal combustion engine operating."
- **Source:** [Pedestrian Safety Enhancement Act of 2010, Pub. L. 111-373](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/PLAW-111publ373/html/PLAW-111publ373.htm), US Congress, signed 2011-01-04 (accessed 2026-10-22)
- **Quote (tool):** the standard must include "requiring new electric or hybrid vehicles to provide an alert sound conforming to the requirements"
- **Scope note on "every":**
  - 4,536 kg = 10,000 lb GVWR. Every electric **car** (sedans, SUVs, light pickups) is covered.
  - Some heavy electric pickups are rated above 10,000 lb. Trade press reports a 10,400 lb GVWR for one launch edition, but I haven't verified that ([The Drive](https://www.thedrive.com/news/40657/the-gmc-hummer-ev-pickup-will-weigh-9046-pounds-report) and others: lead only). Those would fall outside S3(a).
  - So "every new electric car" is ✅. "Every electric vehicle" is ❌.
  - Mild hybrids that can't move without the engine aren't covered.

### B2. Up to about 19 mph (30 km/h); above that, tire and wind noise are loud enough

- **Script (likely):** "It plays up to about 19 miles an hour. Faster than that, your tires and the wind are loud enough on their own."
- **Status:** ✅ verified (tool-quoted; re-quote).
- **Source:** 49 CFR § 571.141 (Cornell LII), as in A4
- **Quote (tool):** S5.1.3: "When at a constant speed greater than 0 km/h but less than 20 km/h." / S5.1.4: "…equal to or greater than 20 km/h but less than 30 km/h." / S5.1.5: "When at a constant speed of 30-32 km/h."
- **Source:** 2016 final rule, as in A3
- **Quote (tool):** "At higher speeds, in contrast, tire and wind noise are the primary contributors to a vehicle's noise output" / "We proposed a crossover speed of 30 km/h"
- **Source:** Pub. L. 111-373, as in B1
- **Quote (tool):** "cross-over speed" means "the speed at which tire noise, wind resistance, or other factors eliminate the need for a separate alert sound"
- **Source (what a car does above it):** [Nissan LEAF Owner's Manual 2023: Approaching Vehicle Sound for Pedestrians (VSP) system](https://www.nissan.com.mt/ownership/owners-manuals/manuals.html/iom/leaf/0ze1/e0/2023/approaching-vehicle-sound-for-pedestrians-vsp-system-1.shtml), Nissan (Malta site), 2023 (accessed 2026-10-22)
- **Quote (tool):** "The sound stops when the vehicle speed exceeds approximately 30 km/h (19 MPH)." / "The sound (re-)starts when the vehicle speed drops below approximately 25 km/h (16 MPH)."
- **Note:**
  - 30 km/h = 18.6 mph. Siemens (A1) states "under 31 km/h (18.6 miles per hour)" for FMVSS 141.
  - The US rule doesn't **forbid** sound above 30 km/h; it just stops requiring it. "Fades" or "switches off" describes what cars do (one maker's manual), not the law. Safe wording: "it's only required up to about 19 miles an hour" or "most cars switch it off above about 19 mph" (🟡: one manual plus Siemens' general statement).

### B3. It must play when stopped in gear and in reverse, but not in Park

- **Script (likely):** "It even plays while you're stopped at a light, and when you back up."
- **Status:** ✅ verified (tool-quoted; re-quote). This **corrects the brief**: NHTSA didn't drop the stationary requirement; it exempted Park.
- **Source:** 49 CFR § 571.141 (Cornell LII), as in A4
- **Quote (tool):** S5.1.1: "When stationary the vehicle must satisfy S5.1.1.1 and S5.1.1.2 whenever the vehicle's propulsion system is activated." / the gear condition: "the vehicle's gear selector is in Neutral or any gear position other than Park that provides forward vehicle propulsion." / S5.1.2: "whenever the vehicle's gear selector is in the Reverse position."
- **Source:** 2016 final rule, as in A3
- **Quote (tool):** "We have retained the requirement that EVs and HVs must generate an alert when stationary" / "the final rule requires an alert only when a vehicle's transmission gear selector is not in the "Park" position."
- **Source:** [Minimum Sound Requirements for Hybrid and Electric Vehicles; response to petitions for reconsideration (FR Doc. 2018-03721)](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2018-02-26/html/2018-03721.htm), NHTSA, 2018-02-26 (accessed 2026-10-22)
- **Quote (tool):** "requires hybrid and electric vehicles to emit sound at minimum levels while the vehicle is stationary" / "(although not when the vehicle is parked, i.e., when the transmission is in "park")"
- **Note:** The European rule (R138) doesn't require a stationary sound. Siemens lists only 10 and 20 km/h forward and 6 km/h reverse. That makes the US stop-light hum a US-specific detail. Not needed in the voice.

### B4. US drivers can't switch it off or turn it down

- **Script (likely):** "And there's no off switch."
- **Status:** ✅ verified (tool-quoted; re-quote) for the US. **Scope matters:** a European LEAF manual describes a driver-operated off switch, "should only be switched off in very unusual cases" (E2). So say "in the US".
- **Source:** Pub. L. 111-373, as in B1
- **Quote (tool):** the standard must "prohibit manufacturers from providing any mechanism for anyone other than the manufacturer or the dealer" / "to disable, alter, replace, or modify the sound or set of sounds" / and "shall not require either driver or pedestrian activation of the alert sound"
- **Source:** 2016 final rule, as in A3 (p. 90420–21 per tool)
- **Quote (tool):** NHTSA may not permit "an off switch or volume control switch that allows the driver to turn off or turn down the alert sound"
- **Source:** 2018 reconsideration, as in B3
- **Quote (tool):** "a vehicle's owner-accessible setup menus should not include a setting that disables the alert system"
- **Source:** 49 CFR § 571.141 S8 (Cornell LII), as in A4
- **Quote (tool):** "(b) Provide any person with any mechanism, equipment, process, or device intended to disable, alter, replace, or modify the sound emitting capability of a vehicle subject to this standard, except in connection with a repair of vehicle malfunction or to remedy a defect or non-compliance."

## C. What the sound is

### C1. The sound is designed: carmakers choose it, within minimum loudness levels across set frequency bands

- **Script (likely):** "That sound isn't the motor. It's designed, like a ringtone, and each carmaker picks its own."
- **Status:** ✅ for "designed / carmakers choose it within legal limits" (Siemens read directly). ✅ (tool-quoted) for the band rule.
  - "Like a ringtone" is 🎭, a comparison.
  - Keep the frequency detail visual, not spoken. Bands: 315 to 5,000 Hz, about the range of a human voice and a little above; that comparison is our gloss, ❌ to speak.
- **Source:** Siemens white paper, as in A1
- **Quote:** "If the additional sound is broadcast through a loudspeaker, no mechanical constraints apply. OEMs are free to design the sound they want within certain legal boundaries." / "An electric vehicle's warning sound is a branding opportunity and can therefore be brand- or even model-defining." / "Apart from a few dominant tones, the EM noise is rather low. Hence, there is a need for an effective warning sound system."
- **Source:** 49 CFR § 571.141 (Cornell LII), as in A4
- **Quote (tool):** S5.1.1.1: "in each of four non-adjacent bands spanning no fewer than 9 of the 13 bands from 315 to 5000 Hz." / band definition: "one of thirteen one-third octave bands having nominal center frequencies ranging from 315 to 5000Hz." / S5.2 (alternative): "two non-adjacent one-third octave bands from 315 to 3150 Hz."
- **Source:** Pub. L. 111-373, as in B1
- **Quote (tool):** the Secretary must "determine the performance requirements for an alert sound that is recognizable to a pedestrian as a motor vehicle in operation"
- **Note:**
  - The rule sets a **minimum** level in several separate pitch bands. A single beep or pure tone won't pass; the sound has to be spread across low and high pitches. That's the checkable reason they sound like a hum or whir rather than a tone.
  - Example minimums (four-band option): 39–42 dB(A) per band when stationary, up to 47–59 at 30 km/h (Table 1–5 values; tool-read, re-check before speaking any number).

### C2. In the US, the sound must get louder as the car speeds up (volume, not pitch)

- **Script (likely):** "As the car speeds up, the sound gets louder, so you can hear it coming faster."
- **Status:** ✅ verified (tool-quoted; re-quote).
- **Source:** 49 CFR § 571.141 (Cornell LII), as in A4
- **Quote (tool):** S5.4: "shall change in volume, as calculated in S7.6, from one critical operating condition to the next."
- **Source:** 2016 final rule, as in A3
- **Quote (tool):** "This requirement was referred to as "pitch shifting," and it is not required in the final rule." / instead "requiring vehicle-emitted sound to increase in sound pressure level by a specified amount as the vehicle's speed increases."
- **Note:**
  - The law asks the sound to let pedestrians tell "constant speed, accelerating, and decelerating" apart (Pub. L. 111-373 via the tool; Siemens p. 4 says the same).
  - The minimum levels also rise step by step: stationary → 10 → 20 → 30 km/h (C1 tables).

### C3. Many carmakers' sounds also rise in pitch with speed, and the UN/European rule tests for it

- **Script (likely):** "Many also rise in pitch, like an engine revving."
- **Status:** 🟡 plausible. Siemens (read directly) says the dominant design method changes pitch with speed and that R138 tests a pitch shift. There's no source that US cars specifically do it. Attribute or hedge ("many"). Don't say "the law makes the pitch rise" for the US (❌, see C2).
- **Source:** Siemens white paper, as in A1
- **Quote:** granular synthesis "is mostly used for AVAS sounds" … grains "are replayed for a certain duration, one after the other, and changed in pitch with vehicle speed." / R138 tests "A frequency shift test where increasing pitch of a particular tone at different speeds allows VRUs to identify if an HEV is accelerating, and, if it is, how fast is it going"

### C4. Every car of the same model and trim must sound the same; drivers can't pick a sound

- **Script (likely):** "Every car of the same model has to sound the same, so you can't swap in your own."
- **Status:** ✅ verified (tool-quoted; re-quote).
- **Source:** 49 CFR § 571.141 (Cornell LII), as in A4
- **Quote (tool):** S5.5.1: "Any two vehicles of the same make, model, model year, body type, and trim level … shall be designed to have the same pedestrian alert sound when operating under the same test conditions and at the same speed"
- **Source:** [Minimum Sound Requirements for Hybrid and Electric Vehicles; final rule (FR Doc. 2022-14733)](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2022-07-13/html/2022-14733.htm), NHTSA, 2022-07-13 (accessed 2026-10-22)
- **Quote (tool):** "The agency has chosen not to adopt the remaining portions of the NPRM," / "including a proposal which would have allowed manufactures of hybrid and electric vehicles (HEVs)" / "to install a number of driver-selectable pedestrian alert sounds in each HEV they manufacture." / "The driver-selectable alert sounds proposal is not being adopted because of a lack of supporting data."
- **Note:** A nice twist: in 2019 NHTSA proposed letting drivers choose among sounds, then dropped it in 2022.

### C5. Surprise: one carmaker let owners play custom sounds through the pedestrian speaker; a recall of 578,607 cars disabled it while driving

- **Script (likely):** "One carmaker let owners play joke sounds through that speaker while driving. Regulators made it switch that off in more than half a million cars."
- **Status:** ✅ verified (tool-quoted from the NHTSA recalls API; re-quote) for the recall facts.
  - "Joke sounds" is 🟡. The NHTSA summary says the feature plays "sound" (custom sounds); the novelty examples come from press coverage only (search snippets).
  - Safer spoken: "custom sounds".
- **Source:** [NHTSA recall 22V-063 (recalls API record)](https://api.nhtsa.gov/recalls/campaignNumber?campaignNumber=22V063), NHTSA, report received 2022-02-04 (the API gives "04/02/2022", day first) (accessed 2026-10-22)

  - Summary: the "Boombox" feature "can play sound through an external speaker while driving", which "may obscure" the Pedestrian Warning System sounds, so the cars fail FMVSS 141.

- **Lead (not opened):** [Part 573 report 22V063](https://static.nhtsa.gov/odi/rcl/2022/RCLRPT-22V063-8773.PDF) (PDF text would not extract).
- **Note:**
  - **Company rule:** the story here is about **one maker's own product feature**, so naming it is allowed by CLAUDE.md, but it isn't needed. Recommendation: keep it unnamed ("one carmaker"). Viewers who know will know.

## D. Why it exists

### D1. NHTSA data: hybrids had higher odds of hitting a pedestrian than gas cars, most of all at low speed

- **Script (likely):** "Federal crash data found hybrids were more likely than gas cars to hit pedestrians, and the gap was biggest at low speeds: turning, pulling out, backing up."
- **Status:** ✅ verified (tool-quoted; re-quote) **as attributed** to NHTSA's 2011 update, as cited in the 2016 rule.
  - Scope: hybrids (not EVs), state crash files from 16 states, odds ratios. A number is allowed only with that scope: "19 percent higher odds".
  - The later low-speed odds ratio of 1.52 (NHTSA research note DOT HS 812 371, Feb 2017) is a search snippet only; the PDF wouldn't extract. 🟡, don't speak.
- **Source:** 2016 final rule, as in A3 (p. 90421 per tool)
- **Quote (tool):** "19-percent higher for pedestrian crash odds and 38-percent higher for bicycle crash odds." / the largest differences occur with "speed limits of 35 mph and lower" / and in "certain maneuvers typically executed at low speed such as making a turn, starting up, and pulling into or backing out of a parking space." / the update increased the states "from 12 to 16"
- **Lead:** [Incidence Rates of Pedestrian and Bicyclist Crashes by Hybrid Electric Passenger Vehicles: An Update (DOT HS 811 526)](https://crashstats.nhtsa.dot.gov/Api/Public/ViewPublication/811526), NHTSA, 2011-10 (not opened). A secondary summary says 35% and 57% from that report. **Conflict:** the 2016 rule's 19%/38% differs from that summary's 35%/57% (different model specs or subsets). Use only the rule's figure, attributed, or no number.
- **Recommendation:** say it without a number: "crash data showed hybrids hit pedestrians more often than gas cars, mostly at low speed" (✅ attributed: "federal crash data").

### D2. Blind pedestrians listen to traffic; the law was written for them

- **Script (likely):** "People who are blind cross streets by listening to traffic, and a car with no engine noise is invisible to them."
- **Status:**
  - ✅ (tool-quoted) for "the law and rule aim at blind and visually impaired pedestrians".
  - 🟡 for the NFB's role and wording: nfb.org returned 403, so it's search snippets only.
  - "Invisible to them" is a 🎭 image; prefer "silent to them".
- **Source:** Pub. L. 111-373, as in B1
- **Quote (tool):** "establishing performance requirements for an alert sound that allows blind and other pedestrians to reasonably detect" / "alert sound" means "a vehicle-emitted sound to enable pedestrians to discern vehicle presence, direction, location, and operation;"
- **Source:** 2016 final rule, as in A3
- **Quote (tool):** "To reduce the risk of pedestrian crashes, especially for the blind and visually-impaired" / "would help blind and visually-impaired pedestrians be aware of nearby vehicles"
- **Lead (🟡):** [President Signs Pedestrian Safety Enhancement Act](https://nfb.org/node/5096), National Federation of the Blind, 2011-01-05 (403; search snippet): "Because blind pedestrians cannot locate and evaluate traffic using their vision, they must listen to traffic" / "New vehicles that employ hybrid or electric engine technology can be silent".
