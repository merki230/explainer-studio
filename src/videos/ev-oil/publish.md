## Description (YouTube)

Why does only the gas car need engine oil changes?

Hundreds of moving parts on one side, about one on the other, and that is only half of the answer.

How many miles do you go between oil changes?

A gas engine's oil keeps its metal parts from rubbing and carries heat away, and the engine's own combustion slowly wears that oil out. Engineers call the gas that leaks past the pistons into the oil "blow-by": it carries carbon (soot), water, acids and partly burned fuel, and the acids use up the oil's additives while fuel thins it. An EV motor has no combustion and one moving part, the rotor, so there is no engine oil to change. An EV does have gearbox oil (Tesla's service manual lists about 2.3 liters in a Model 3's rear drive unit), but Tesla's published service schedule lists no change for it. That same schedule still lists a brake-fluid check, a cabin filter and tire rotations.

Notes on the numbers:
- "Hundreds of moving parts" covers a gas car's engine plus transmission. Published estimates run from about 200 (drivetrain) to 2,000 or more; none is a primary engineering count, so treat it as an estimate. The EV figure is for the motor only: one, the rotor.
- "Dozens of metal parts" inside the engine is our own count of one four-cylinder engine's main moving parts (about 75: pistons, rods, wrist pins, rings, crankshaft, camshafts, valves, springs, lifters, before the timing chain and pumps), not a published figure.
- "Five to ten thousand miles" is a common range for many modern cars; your owner's manual rules.
- The consumer study is Consumer Reports' 2020 survey of owners, which grouped battery EVs with plug-in hybrids and counted maintenance and repair, not only oil. It is reported here through Green Car Reports.

Illustrative: the cars (generic, not any real model), the people and the shop are drawn examples. The magnified oil film and piston-ring gap are drawn larger than real so you can see them. The oil and particle colors are a drawing, not lab color. The combustion flash is a drawing, slowed down. The oil-life gauge is generic, not a real car's. The dimming orange line is a drawing of the oil's protection wearing down, not something you can see in a real engine. The coat of oil on the gearbox gears is a drawing. The ghost drain pan under the EV is a drawing of a job that isn't there.

(How the video's wording maps to these. Tesla service intervals: brake-fluid health check every 4 years, cabin air filter every 2 years, tire rotation every 6,250 miles; the page lists no gearbox oil, coolant or engine oil. It says the intervals are based on typical driving, so the video says the schedule "doesn't list a change", not that the oil never needs one. Tesla service manual: the rear drive unit takes 2.3 L of gearbox fluid with a pump; the video rounds to "about two liters", and newer rear units may differ slightly. Spectro Scientific: blow-by gases "contain particles of carbon, water, acids, partially burned fuels", additives neutralize acids and deplete, fuel dilution thins the oil; additives are also loaded by other things such as soot, so the video's "the acid uses up the additives" is the main mechanism, not the only one. NAPA: oil keeps metal from rubbing and carries heat away. CalRecycle: the old 3,000-mile rule is out of date and many cars go 5,000 to 15,000 miles; KBB: roughly 5,000 to 7,500 miles on conventional oil, 7,500 to 10,000 or more on synthetic. Explain that Stuff and Mazda USA: an electric motor has one moving part, the rotor, turning inside the stator's coils. AFDC: an EV has fewer fluids, such as engine oil, that need regular maintenance. Green Car Reports on Consumer Reports: 50% less over the vehicle's life (defined as 200,000 miles), from owner surveys, battery EVs and plug-in hybrids together. Green Car Future and CarBuzz: the parts-count estimates behind "hundreds"; the video uses the low end and scopes it to engine plus transmission.)
