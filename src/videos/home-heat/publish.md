## Description (YouTube)

How can the air outside hold any heat when it's 30 degrees?

A heat pump puts more heat into the house than the electricity it uses, and the missing heat comes from somewhere you would not expect.

Does your house make its heat, or move it?

Illustrative: the house, the cold night, the 9:40 PM time and the person on the couch are an example, not a real home or a real person. Our house stands in for the Canadian test house, which was an unoccupied research house in Ottawa. 30 degrees is an example outdoor temperature, not a measurement. The outdoor temperature moving up and down like a dial is a drawing, not a recording. The thicker coil tubes are drawn to show where the liquid boils, not how a real coil is built. Electric backup strips are the one kind of backup heat shown; real homes may have a gas furnace instead, or no backup at all. The parked EV is generic, not a real model. The orange flows are drawings of heat and of the energy spent making or moving it, not measurements. A space heater, a heat pump and backup strips in one house is a composite for the picture; few homes have all three running at once. The numbers on screen (about 3 and 1.5 units of heat per unit of electricity, 5 above and 13 below zero, 10 to 20 degrees colder) are from the sources below.

A heat pump moves heat from outside to inside instead of making it, which is why one unit of electricity can bring in about three units of heat on a mild night. The 3 and 1.5 figures come from one cold-climate heat pump in one unoccupied test house in Ottawa, so other models and homes will differ. Heat pumps also periodically defrost the outdoor coil in cold, damp weather (Natural Resources Canada describes this), which the video does not show, and 30 degrees is only an example outdoor temperature.

(How the video's wording maps to these. "Heat always flows from warmer to colder" is the second-law statement in OpenStax 15.3, and "the extra heat was already outside" and "the electricity runs the compressor, and the rest of the heat comes in from outside" are the heat-pump energy balance in OpenStax 15.5. "Even 30-degree air holds heat" rests on NRCan's statement that a good deal of energy is still available in cold air, and on IEEE Spectrum's "there is heat in the air even in subzero temperatures"; 30 degrees is an example inside that range. "Usually 10 to 20 degrees colder" is ACHR News's "typically 10-20 degrees F colder". "Boils into a gas" and "a compressor squeezes the gas, which heats it up" are NRCan's description of the cycle, and the compression heating is also in OpenStax University Physics 3.6. "One unit for each unit of electricity" for a space heater is a COP of 1, per Canary Media's reporting on the Joule study. "One Canadian government test house" with "about three" at a mild 40 degrees and "one and a half" at 6 below is the Sager, Armstrong and Szadkowski test of one cold-climate unit in an unoccupied house: average COP of 3.0 at 41 degrees F and 1.5 at minus 6 degrees F. "Most of them shut off", "newer heat pumps ... between 5 above and 13 below zero" and "backup heat, if there is any, takes over" are NRCan's minimum operating temperature (minus 15 to minus 25 degrees C for newer models, which is 5 to minus 13 degrees F) and its supplementary heating; the range is a spread of settings, not one value. The electric car line is Recurrent's and fueleconomy.gov's: some heaters move heat from outside air, others draw energy from the battery to make it, and which one a car has is the maker's choice.)
